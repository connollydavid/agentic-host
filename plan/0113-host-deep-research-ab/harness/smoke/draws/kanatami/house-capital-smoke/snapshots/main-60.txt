    Paris, Banks of the Seine
      Paris, Banks of the Seine (French: Paris, rives de la Seine) is a UNESCO World Heritage property covering the bridges, quays and banks of the River Seine in central Paris, together with the Île de la Cité and the Île Saint-Louis. Inscribed in 1991 under cultural criteria (i), (ii) and (iv), it stretches along the historic course of the river between the Pont de Sully and the Pont d'Iéna and was extended by an approved minor boundary modification in 2024.1 • 2 • 3

      What the property contains
      The city of Paris is built along a bend in the Seine, between the confluences of the Marne and the Oise. The inscribed property takes in the river's historic course, its quays and bridges, and the two central islands. UNESCO's statement of Outstanding Universal Value describes the ensemble as a geographical and historical entity in which the successive layers of Paris's history are superposed along a single river landscape.2
      
      
      The monuments along the banks span nearly eight centuries of building. The Cathedral of Notre-Dame and the Sainte-Chapelle are masterpieces of the Middle Ages; the Pont Neuf illustrates the spirit of the French Renaissance; the coherent districts of the Marais and the Île Saint-Louis, along with the Quai Malaquais and the Quai Voltaire, testify to Parisian town planning of the 17th and 18th centuries; and the riverfront carries the major works of French classicism, including the Louvre, the Hôtel des Invalides, the École Militaire and the Monnaie (the Mint). Buildings from the Universal Exhibitions of the 19th and 20th centuries line the river as well, headed by the Eiffel Tower, described by UNESCO as a universally recognized icon of Paris and of iron architecture, together with the Grand and Petit Palais, the Pont Alexandre III and the Palais de Chaillot.2
      Why it was inscribed
      The property meets three of UNESCO's cultural criteria. Under criterion (i), the banks hold a succession of architectural and urban masterpieces built from the Middle Ages to the 20th century, from Notre-Dame and the Sainte-Chapelle through the Louvre, the Palais de l'Institut, the Place de la Concorde and the Grand Palais to the Eiffel Tower and the Palais de Chaillot. Under criterion (ii), buildings such as Notre-Dame and the Sainte-Chapelle became the source from which Gothic architecture spread, the Place de la Concorde and the Invalides vista influenced the urban development of European capitals, and Haussmann's planning, which marks the western part of the city, inspired the construction of great cities of the New World, particularly in Latin America. Under criterion (iv), the monuments and representative buildings, united by the river landscape, illustrate most of the styles, decorative arts and building methods employed over nearly eight centuries.2
      The choice of the stretch between the Pont de Sully and the Pont d'Iéna follows an old distinction along the river: upstream, the port and river transport; downstream, royal and aristocratic Paris. It is the downstream section, where the functions of the capital developed, that was inscribed. The historic city itself developed between the 16th and 20th centuries, and the Seine had played both a defensive and an economic role since the first settlements, from prehistoric times to the Parisii.2
      Protection and management
      The property enjoys what UNESCO describes as the highest level of legal protection under the French Heritage Code, Town Planning Code and Environment Code. It sits within an inscribed site and further comprises six listed sites, notably the Invalides, the Champ de Mars and the Jardins du Trocadéro, and all its important monuments are fully listed as Historic Monuments. The French State owns the quays (the fluvial public domain) and most of the monuments, while the city of Paris owns the public areas, the Hôtel de Ville, the parish churches and numerous other plots.2
      No management plan or management authority is specifically devoted to the World Heritage property; instead, management by owners and tenants is scientifically and technically controlled by the State through the legal protections in place.2 Two reference documents govern the riverfront: the 1999 Cahier des prescriptions urbaines et paysagères, prepared jointly by the city, the State and the autonomous Port, and the 2015 Specifications for seasonal installations, which control temporary use of the lower quays.2 The World Heritage Committee has repeatedly pressed for a fuller framework: in 2021 it declined a proposed boundary modification and referred the buffer zone back to France, and in 2023 it noted the need to create an integrated management plan, recommending interim plans for each component first.6 • 7 As of 2026, Le Monde reported that such a document still does not exist for the banks, and that throughout 2024 the city and the State had agreed to develop one for the capital.4
      The riverfront in use
      The lower quays were definitively closed to automobile traffic over nearly the whole property, since 2014 on the Left Bank and 2016 on the Right Bank, a change UNESCO credits with helping to preserve the site's authenticity and integrity.2 Since 2017 the right-bank quays between the Tuileries and the Pont de Sully have been fully pedestrianized, giving a 3.3-kilometer car-free promenade.8 The pedestrianization of part of the riverbank had begun earlier, in 2011 between the Musée d'Orsay and the Alma bridge, and provoked a public dispute between the mayor of Paris, Bertrand Delanoë, and Prime Minister François Fillon.9
      UNESCO's assessment notes that the urban and visual integrity of the site, its wide perspectives from the banks, is vulnerable to urban development pressure, traffic pollution and tourism, and requires rigorous control. The completion and consolidation of the riverside vistas dates from the first half of the 20th century, with the Palais de Tokyo and the Palais de Chaillot.2
      Notre-Dame and recent committee decisions
      The fire of 15 April 2019 damaged Notre-Dame Cathedral, a component of the property, and the World Heritage Committee expressed its solidarity with France at its 2019 session.10 Restoration has proceeded in phases: the second phase was completed in 2025, including the covering of the spire, the reinstallation of the statues of the apostles and the tetramorph in July 2025, and the reopening of the towers' visitor circuit in September 2025, with further work focused on the chevet and sacristy along the river.5 • 11
      At its 48th session the Committee noted with concern a project to install contemporary stained-glass windows in six chapels of the cathedral's southern nave, judging that the Heritage Impact Assessment's reading of the property's Outstanding Universal Value may not fully acknowledge Notre-Dame's role as a key attribute, and asked France to suspend the project and reconsider replacing the historic windows. It also requested an Integrated Management Plan including a Risk Prevention and Management Plan, and an updated state of conservation report by 1 February 2027 for examination at the Committee's 49th session.5
