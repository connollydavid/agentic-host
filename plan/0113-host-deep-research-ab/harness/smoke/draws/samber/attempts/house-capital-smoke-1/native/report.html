Type: domain (general-reference fact check; no built-in type fit, axes derived from the question) | Date: 2026-11-27 | Constraints: none

Assumptions: depth = Quick (narrow, well-scoped factual question → Steps 1, 2, 5); scope = metropolitan France, present day (Paris); "river that runs through it" = the principal river crossing the capital city, with secondary watercourses noted for completeness.

Domain Overview

Capital of France

The capital of France is Paris, and French state references state it in exactly those terms. The Ministry of Culture's official terminology database lists, under "France (la)", the field Capitale: "Paris" FranceTerme (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The Foreign Ministry's capital recommendation published in the Journal officiel of 24 September 2008 gives "Paris" on the France row Légifrance — Recommandation (JORF 24/09/2008) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary), and the National Toponymy Commission's reference list, validated in plenary on 16 September 2025 and described as the common reference for the public and the administration, records "France (la) — PARIS" CNIG/IGN, Pays, territoires et villes du monde (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The Interior Ministry's civic-training module for foreign residents states the proposition flatly — "Paris est la capitale de la France" Ministère de l'Intérieur, module PDF (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary) — as does the state tourism operator france.fr (tier: Primary). Five independent Primary sources agree, so this claim sits at High confidence.

The interesting nuance is the legal basis, not the answer: no French constitution names a capital. Article 2 of the Constitution of 4 October 1958 enumerates the emblem, anthem, motto and (since 1992) the language, and says nothing about a capital Sénat — Constitution du 4 octobre 1958 (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Public-law scholarship is consistent: Olivier Renaudie notes in the Revue du droit public that "Paris est ignoré par le texte de la Constitution de 1958", contrasting Spain (art. 5, Madrid) and Germany (Basic Law art. 22, Berlin), and concluding that the statute of Paris is "laissée à la discrétion du législateur" Renaudie, Revue du droit public 2019/6 (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established). Giacomo Delledonne goes further, holding that the capital is not mentioned in any constitution enacted since 1791 Delledonne, Perspectives on Federalism 16(3), 2024 (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established); a comparative EU study adds that the long list of towns that have performed capital functions — Orléans, Troyes, Versailles, Bordeaux, Vichy — has never made Paris's position questionable Grabowski, Przegląd Prawa Konstytucyjnego 2017 (tier: Established).

The binding basis is therefore ordinary statute fixing the seat of public powers. Article 1 of Ordonnance n° 58-1100 of 17 November 1958 reads "L'Assemblée nationale et le Sénat siègent à Paris," with a second paragraph allowing the seat of public powers to be moved to another town if circumstances require Assemblée nationale — Ordonnance 58-1100; Sénat — pouvoirs publics (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). This descends from the settlement of 1879: Article 9 of the constitutional law of 25 February 1875 had provided that "Le siège du pouvoir exécutif et des deux chambres est à Versailles" — the only time a French constitutional text fixed the seat, and it fixed it outside Paris — before being abrogated by the constitutional law of 21 June 1879 Élysée — Les lois constitutionnelles de 1875 (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary).

The principal national institutions all sit in Paris: the presidency at the Élysée Élysée — 300 ans d'histoire élyséenne (tier: Primary), the head of government at Matignon, "assigned to the head of government since 1935" info.gouv.fr — Le Premier ministre (tier: Primary), the National Assembly at the Palais-Bourbon Assemblée nationale and the Senate at the Luxembourg, "Siège de la seconde chambre depuis 1799" Sénat (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). One real carve-out: the Parliament meeting as Congress still sits in the chamber at the Palace of Versailles under Article 2 of the same ordinance Sénat, Rapport n° 314 (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). This suggests a residual "second seat" in French practice — Versailles for the constituent assembly, Paris for everything else.

Historical exceptions are documented but were transfers of the seat, not changes of capital. The palace's own operator dates the court's installation at Versailles to 6 May 1682 and stresses the town became the kingdom's governmental centre "sans que Paris ne perde son titre de capitale" Château de Versailles (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Crisis relocations followed a recognisable pattern: Bordeaux from 12 February 1871 and Versailles from 20 March 1871, despite Louis Blanc's address demanding the government declare it "regarde Paris comme la capitale nécessaire de la France" Assemblée nationale — Louis Blanc (10 mars 1871) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); the 1914 Bordeaux government; and the 1940 chain out of Paris ending at Vichy Sénat, Rapport n° 314 (tier: Primary). Because Paris's status rests on ordinary law, it was never formally surrendered in any of these episodes.

Conflicts noted (capital axis): the Council of Europe's comparative report frames Paris as a "conventional capital without legal recognition", while French primary law legally fixes the seat of the executive and both chambers in Paris — reconcilable only if "capital" means a formal label, since no adopted French text appears to apply the word capitale to Paris. Dates of the 1870–71 Bordeaux episode differ between a regional feature (9 Dec 1870 – 10 Mar 1871) and the parliamentary histories (Assembly at Bordeaux from 12 Feb 1871); the parliamentary dates are used. Gaps: no French statute or constitutional revision found that names Paris "capitale" (searched in French and English); no primary decree located for the 1914 or 1940 seat transfers; no official source found designating Brazzaville, Algiers or Bayeux as seat of Free France/the provisional government (only Wikipedia-type accounts surfaced, not used); Encyclopædia Britannica returned HTTP 403.

River running through the capital

The river that runs through Paris is the Seine (la Seine). The French interior ministry's official civic-geography atlas describes it as rising "en Bourgogne, près de Dijon" and emptying into the English Channel, at 776 km Atlas des fleuves français, Ministère de l'Intérieur (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The City of Paris presents the Seine as its own river — "La Seine, cœur battant de Paris" — giving 777 km "entre le plateau de Langres et l'estuaire du Havre" Ville de Paris (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). UNESCO's World Heritage description independently fixes both halves of the question in one sentence: Paris is "the capital city of one of the first great nation states of Europe," built "along a bend in the River Seine, between the confluence of the Marne and the Oise Rivers" UNESCO World Heritage Centre, ref. 600 (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The name derives from Sequana, the Gallo-Roman goddess of the river, whose sanctuary stood at the source Ville de Paris (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary).

Source, course and mouth. The Seine rises in the commune of Source-Seine (Côte-d'Or, Bourgogne-Franche-Comté) on the Plateau de Langres, about 30 km north-west of Dijon, and flows generally south-east to north-west across the Paris Basin to the English Channel between Le Havre and Honfleur (corroborated by Britannica Students, tier: Established; Agence de l'eau Seine-Normandie and Ville de Paris, both tier: Primary; confidence: High). An administrative detail worth noting: the source itself is owned by the City of Paris, which bought the site on 6 April 1864 under Haussmann and opened a landscaped grotto designed by Baltard and Davioud in 1867 Ville de Paris (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Along its course it drains the sedimentary Paris Basin on a low-energy, meandering reach and receives the Yonne, Marne and Oise — the last two just upstream and downstream of Paris respectively Agence de l'eau Seine-Normandie (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary).

Length. Sources disagree within a narrow band, so the figure must be hedged. The national water-reference database Sandre records 775 km Sandre/EauFrance, 2021 Wayback capture (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); the interior ministry atlas and Encyclopædia Universalis give 776 km (tiers: Primary, Established); Ville de Paris and the Seine-Normandy agency give 777 km; a peer-reviewed UNESCO-published chapter by Lestel, Meybeck, Carré and Belliard gives 775 km, decomposed as 607 km river plus a 170-km estuarial reach Lestel et al., River Culture, UNESCO Publishing 2023 (tier: Primary); outliers run from 754 km (EU JRC basin fact sheet, tier: Primary) to 781 km (Britannica's 485 mi). This suggests the defensible statement is 775–777 km, with the spread explained by dataset vintage and whether the tidal estuary is counted (confidence: High).

The Seine inside Paris. Within the city the river runs 13 km, is 30 m wide at quai Montebello and about 200 m at the pont de Grenelle, 3.40–5.70 m deep, and is crossed by 37 bridges including 4 pedestrian footbridges — figures repeated by Ville de Paris Paris et ses ponts and by the Centre des monuments nationaux's official Sainte-Chapelle dossier PDF (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). It divides the city into the Rive Droite to the north and the Rive Gauche to the south, the naming following the direction of flow as the river runs westward Guichet du Savoir, BML and Paris je t'aime (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tiers: Established, Primary). Two islands sit in its channel — the Île de la Cité and the Île Saint-Louis, both within the UNESCO property, the latter artificially joined in the 17th century UNESCO; Encyclopædia Universalis (confidence: High). The banks between Pont de Sully and Pont d'Iéna are inscribed as "Paris, Banks of the Seine" (ref. 600, 1991) UNESCO (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary).

Flood regime. The level at Paris is gauged permanently at Austerlitz (station F700000103), forecast by the State's SPC Seine Moyenne–Yonne–Loing and relayed by the Préfecture de Police Ville de Paris, Crues and Vigicrues (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The reference flood remains January 1910, peak 8.62 m at Austerlitz, mitigated by four upstream reservoir dams built from 1949 Episeine; Encyclopædia Universalis (confidence: High). Mean discharge at Paris is of the order of 300 m³/s (sources range 280–330 m³/s depending on station and averaging period).

Conflicts noted (Seine axis): length 754 / 773 / 775 / 776 / 777 / 781 km; source elevation 446 / 447 / 450 / 452 m vs legacy 471 m; departments crossed 12 / 13 / 14; catchment 75,000–79,000 km²; discharge at Paris 280–330 m³/s and at the mouth 450–611 m³/s; 1910 peak discharge 2,400 vs ~2,600 m³/s; bridge count 37 (incl. 4 footbridges) vs 38 + 3 (Low-tier source, Bonjour RATP). Gaps: live Sandre card is JS-only (a 2021 Wayback capture was used); Britannica's main Seine article returned HTTP 403, so Britannica Students was used via a search excerpt (Medium confidence); the peer-reviewed basis for the City's "≈14,000-year-old course" claim was not retrieved.

Cross-checks and competing claims

The core answer was tested against sources chosen to disagree with it, and none does. France's state tourism portal describes Paris as "la capitale française" set "en bord de Seine" (France.fr) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); the Interior Ministry's civic atlas lists the Seine's "grandes villes traversées" as Troyes, Paris, Rouen and Le Havre (Ministère de l'Intérieur, Formation civique) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); the City of Paris states the Seine "fait vibrer la capitale le long de ses 37 ponts répartis sur les 13 kilomètres qu'elle parcourt intra-muros" (Ville de Paris) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); UNESCO's inscription states "la ville de Paris est construite dans un méandre de la Seine entre la confluence de la Marne et celle de l'Oise" and calls Paris "ville capitale de l'un des premiers grands États-nations d'Europe" (UNESCO WHC) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); and Larousse independently gives "Capitale de la France, chef-lieu de la Région Île-de-France, sur la Seine", adding that Paris has held that role "depuis Hugues Capet (fin du Xe siècle)" (Larousse) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established). Government, municipal, intergovernmental, scientific and encyclopedic sources in two languages place the claim well past the two-source threshold.

Alternative rivers a careful analyst must rule out. The Marne and Oise are tributaries, not the river of Paris: the Marne joins at the city's south-eastern edge (Charenton–Alfortville–Ivry) and the Oise about 20 km north-west at Conflans-Sainte-Honorine, which is precisely why UNESCO's formulation brackets Paris between the two confluences, on the Seine proper (AESN; Ministère de l'Intérieur) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The Bièvre is the genuine "lost second river": 36 km long, it ran about 5 km through the 5th and 13th arrondissements to the only natural confluence with the Seine in the city centre, at the Gare d'Austerlitz (Ville de Paris; APUR (PDF)) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary) — but it has not flowed openly in Paris since 1912 and lost, then (outside Paris) in 2007 regained, its status as a watercourse (SMBVB; BnF/Gallica) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High). The canals — l'Ourcq, Saint-Martin (4.5 km, 9 locks) and Saint-Denis — are a 130-km artificial network owned by the City that lets barges "couper une boucle de la Seine" (Ville de Paris; VNF) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary), so naming the Canal Saint-Martin means naming a feeder of the Seine. Peer-reviewed historical geography adds the vanished Petite Seine and the Île Louviers arm infilled in 1844 (Noizet & Gautier, Villes et rivières de France, CNRS Éditions 2019 (PDF)) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The conclusion follows from the evidence rather than from convention: the Seine is the only natural river with an open channel crossing Paris.

Alternative capitals divide into two genuine interruptions and a set of titles that mean something else. Versailles was the "nouveau centre du royaume jusqu'en 1789, sans que Paris ne perde son titre de capitale" (Château de Versailles) (confidence: High, tier: Primary), and the constitutional law of 1875 briefly fixed the seat of the powers there until the loi du 22 juillet 1879 relative au siège du Pouvoir exécutif et des Chambres à Paris reversed it (Légifrance; Sénat) (confidence: High, tier: Primary), Gambetta saluting the return of "le titre légal" (Assemblée nationale, rapport n° 2226) (tier: Primary). Bordeaux served three times (1870–71, Sept–Dec 1914, 1940) (Sénat; Conseil d'État) (confidence: High, tier: Primary), and the 1914 press insisted Paris was "la capitale qu'il n'a pas cessé d'être en droit" (Retronews/BnF) (confidence: High, tier: Established). Vichy is the trickiest competing claim: the Allier departmental archives say the government "choisit Vichy comme capitale" (Archives départementales de l'Allier) (confidence: High, tier: Primary), while the Interior Ministry, Larousse and the USHMM encyclopaedia describe it neutrally as the seat of government (Ministère de l'Intérieur; USHMM) (confidence: High). This suggests the defensible synthesis is de facto seat versus de jure Paris — reported here as a live conflict rather than resolved silently. Free France's Brazzaville, "capitale de la France libre" from 1940 until Algiers in June 1943 (Fondation Charles de Gaulle) (confidence: High, tier: Established), is a belligerent-era claim, not a rival designation.

Two modern confusions to pre-empt. Officials routinely call regional prefectures "capitales régionales" — including Strasbourg, "désignée comme capitale régionale par la loi de 16 janvier 2015" (Stratégie-plan.gouv.fr) (confidence: High, tier: Primary) — a sub-national usage in which Paris is simultaneously national capital and Île-de-France chef-lieu. Strasbourg's "capitale européenne" titles derive from the Council of Europe seat and the 12 December 1992 Edinburgh decision replicated in Protocol No. 6 (Sénat, rapport n° 254; Assemblée nationale, rapport n° 2035 (PDF); Council of Europe (PDF)) (confidence: High, tier: Primary) — seats of European institutions in France, not the capital of France. A related myth, "Lyon, capitale des Gaules", is "un mythe tenace" per historian Patrice Faure (France Culture) (confidence: High, tier: Established).

The best-documented error adjacent to this question is a superlative, not the identity. Many assume the Seine is France's longest river; it is the second, the Loire at ~1,020 km being the longest (Ministère de l'Intérieur) (confidence: High, tier: Primary). Exam-prep sites report candidates mis-picking the Seine precisely "parce qu'elle traverse Paris" (Prépa-examen.fr; Feuille.org) (confidence: Low, tier: Low — single-purpose study sites, cited only as attestation of the misconception, not of the fact). Paris's own heritage portal likewise cautions that the schoolbook source (Langres plateau, 471 m) is "pas faux, mais pas tout à fait vrai" (Patrimoine professionnel, Ville de Paris) (confidence: High, tier: Primary). No major outlet was found publishing the outright errors "France's capital is not Paris" or "a non-Seine river crosses central Paris".

Conflicts noted (cross-check axis): Vichy "capitale" vs "siège du gouvernement" (archives + Joly vs Interior Ministry, Larousse, USHMM); Bièvre burial dates (1912 open-flow end vs 1935 last covered section per APUR vs "1875–1912" in a Low-tier source); Brazzaville capital start (28 Aug vs 26 Oct 1940); "Lyon capitale des Gaules" (Inrap shorthand vs scholarly denial). No consulted source contradicts "Paris" or "the Seine". Gaps: no rigorous public-opinion survey quantifying misidentification of the river (only Low-tier attestations); no non-Wikipedia official source directly confirming constitutional silence (Medium); the 2019 update of the capital-names recommendation was not verified on Légifrance (the 1993 and 2008 texts were).

Key Findings

1. The answer is Paris and the Seine, and the evidence is unusually thick. Two Primary-tier state sources name the pairing in a single statement — the Interior Ministry's civic atlas lists Paris among the Seine's "grandes villes traversées" Ministère de l'Intérieur (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High) and UNESCO's World Heritage description calls Paris "ville capitale" built "dans un méandre de la Seine" UNESCO (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High). Across government, municipal, intergovernmental, peer-reviewed and encyclopedic sources in two languages, not one contradicted either half of the answer. For a question of this kind that is about as strong a corroboration record as exists.

2. Paris's capital status is statutory, not constitutional — the one genuinely non-obvious finding. Article 2 of the 1958 Constitution enumerates the emblem, anthem, motto and language and never mentions a capital Sénat (confidence: High, tier: Primary), a silence public-law scholarship treats as deliberate rather than accidental Renaudie, Revue du droit public 2019/6; Delledonne 2024 (confidence: High). The operative text is Article 1 of Ordonnance n° 58-1100 — "L'Assemblée nationale et le Sénat siègent à Paris" Assemblée nationale (confidence: High, tier: Primary) — descending from the loi du 22 juillet 1879 that abrogated the 1875 clause fixing the seat at Versailles Légifrance (confidence: High, tier: Primary). The designation as capital itself comes from terminology instruments: FranceTerme and the JORF capital list put Paris in the "Capitale" field FranceTerme; Légifrance (confidence: High). This suggests France is an instructive counter-example to countries such as Spain or Germany, whose constitutions do name their capitals.

3. The Seine's identity is settled; its numbers are not. Length appears as 773, 775, 776, 777 and 781 km across the Seine-Normandy agency, Sandre, the Interior Ministry, Ville de Paris and Britannica respectively, with the EU JRC at 754 km. The clustering of official French sources at 775–777 km Sandre/EauFrance; Ministère de l'Intérieur; Ville de Paris (confidence: High) makes that the defensible figure, and the pattern indicates the outliers turn on dataset vintage and whether the tidal estuarial reach is counted — the peer-reviewed Lestel et al. decomposition (607 km river plus 170 km estuary) UNESCO Publishing 2023 (tier: Primary) is the clearest explanation of the spread. The same pattern holds for catchment (75,000–79,000 km²), discharge at Paris (280–330 m³/s) and source elevation (446–452 m against a legacy 471 m).

4. "The Seine" survives every rival answer because it is the only natural river with an open channel in Paris. The Marne and Oise are tributaries joining at Charenton and Conflans AESN (confidence: High, tier: Primary). The Bièvre — the only affluent ever to have had a natural confluence with the Seine inside the city, at Austerlitz — has not flowed openly since 1912 Ville de Paris; APUR (PDF) (confidence: High, tier: Primary). The Ourcq, Saint-Martin and Saint-Denis canals are a 130-km artificial, City-owned network Ville de Paris (confidence: High, tier: Primary). Rival capitals fail on the same logic: Versailles was a seat while Paris kept the title, in the palace's own words Château de Versailles (confidence: High, tier: Primary); Bordeaux and Vichy were wartime seats; Strasbourg's titles are European-institutional Sénat, rapport n° 254 (confidence: High, tier: Primary).

5. The one unresolved conflict is terminological, and it concerns Vichy. The Allier departmental archives state the 1940 government "choisit Vichy comme capitale" Archives départementales de l'Allier (confidence: High, tier: Primary) while the Interior Ministry, Larousse and the USHMM describe it as the seat of government Ministère de l'Intérieur; USHMM (confidence: High). Reported here rather than settled: since the capital is fixed by ordinary law below constitutional level, the legitimacy question that decides it is itself contested historiography. It does not touch the present-day answer.

  4. Pre-empt the Strasbourg and "capitales régionales" confusions when the audience is non-French, since both use the word capitale for something other than the national capital Stratégie-plan.gouv.fr (confidence: High, tier: Primary).
  5. Treat the Bièvre as the useful teaching case, not as an alternative answer. It is a real, culverted Paris river and the strongest candidate a well-read respondent might offer; naming it with its 1912 burial demonstrates command of the material rather than doubt about it.

Risks and Uncertainties

  • Data gaps: no adopted French statute or constitutional revision was found that applies the word capitale to Paris — only terminology and toponymy instruments do, searched in French and English.   • Low-confidence claims needing validation: the prevalence of the "Seine is France's longest river" misconception rests solely on Low-tier study sites.   • Unresolved conflicts: Vichy as "capitale" versus "siège du gouvernement"; the Bièvre's burial dates (1912 vs 1935); Brazzaville's start date (28 Aug vs 26 Oct 1940); the Council of Europe's "conventional capital without legal recognition" framing versus French statutes that legally fix the seat in Paris.
