# Axis notes — Cross-checks, competing claims, and common misconceptions
Topic: Capital of France and the river that runs through it
Date: 2026-11-27 · Analyst: subagent (cross-check axis)

## Cross-checks and competing claims

**The core answer — Paris is the capital of France and the Seine is the river that crosses it — is confirmed by a wide set of mutually independent authoritative references, with no dissent anywhere in the consulted record.** The French state's official tourism portal describes Paris as "la capitale française" set "en bord de Seine" and locates the historic core (Île de la Cité, "berceau de l'antique Lutèce") on the river ([France.fr](https://www.france.fr/fr/destination/paris/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The Ministry of the Interior's official civic-education atlas lists the Seine's "grandes villes traversées" as Troyes, **Paris**, Rouen and Le Havre ([Ministère de l'Intérieur, Formation civique](https://formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr/fiches-par-thematiques/histoire-geographie-et-culture/atlas-de-la-france/les-fleuves-fran%C3%A7ais/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The city's own government states the Seine "fait vibrer la capitale le long de ses 37 ponts répartis sur les 13 kilomètres qu'elle parcourt intra-muros" ([Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/dossiers/la-seine-coeur-battant-de-paris-216) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). UNESCO's World Heritage inscription "Paris, Banks of the Seine" (inscribed 1991) states that "la ville de Paris est construite dans un méandre de la Seine entre la confluence de la Marne et celle de l'Oise" and characterises Paris as "ville capitale de l'un des premiers grands États-nations d'Europe" ([UNESCO WHC](https://whc.unesco.org/fr/list/600) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The Larousse encyclopedia independently gives "Capitale de la France, chef-lieu de la Région Île-de-France, sur la Seine", adding that Paris has been "capitale de la France depuis Hugues Capet (fin du Xe siècle)" ([Larousse](https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/ville/Paris/137068) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established)). The state's own hydrographic registry (Sandre code ----0010) validates "La Seine" as a single named watercourse ([Sandre, via Internet Archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20210123171121/http://www.sandre.eaufrance.fr/geo/CoursEau/----0010) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)), and the Seine-Normandie Water Agency confirms its course from Source-Seine (Côte-d'Or) to the English Channel between Le Havre and Honfleur ([Agence de l'eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/agence-de-leau/le-bassin-de-la-seine) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). Encyclopædia Britannica, English-language, corroborates both halves of the claim: Paris "is the capital of the country of France… The Seine River flows through the city center" ([Britannica Kids](https://kids.britannica.com/kids/article/Paris/345991) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established)). These are government (two ministries, Légifrance), municipal, intergovernmental (UNESCO), scientific (Sandre/water agency), and commercial-encyclopedic sources in two languages — far above the two-source threshold, so the core claim is **confidence: High**. One cross-check caveat: the river's reported total length varies by measurement convention — 773 km (water agency), 775 km (Sandre), 776 km (Ministry of the Interior), 777 km (Paris.fr and France.fr), and 780–781 km / 485 miles (Britannica). Sources disagree numerically but never on identity: all refer to the same fleuve traversing Paris.

**Legally, the claim "Paris is the capital" rests on official terminology and statute rather than on the 1958 Constitution itself.** The Constitution of the Fifth Republic contains no article naming a capital (a point I could locate only on Wikipedia — used as a signpost — and in the contextual corroboration below, so it is stated with **confidence: Medium**). The state's authoritative designation comes instead from the arrêté du 4 novembre 1993 relatif à la terminologie des noms d'États et de capitales (Foreign Ministry and Education Ministry, signed Juppé/Bayrou), whose annexed list — the so-called "arrêté Juppé" — enters, under the column "Capitale ou siège du gouvernement", **Paris** for France; the same list's guiding principle is that "lorsqu'un État est constitué... celle qui est indiquée en priorité est le siège actuel du gouvernement" ([Légifrance, arrêté du 4 novembre 1993](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000000181509) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). It is reconfirmed by the 2008 inter-ministerial recommendation on names of states and capitals, whose table likewise lists "France — Paris" ([Légifrance, recommandation 2008](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000019509867) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). Parliament's own history confirms that since 1879 the seat of the legislature has deliberately been fixed by ordinary law and not by constitutional text — the National Assembly's 2005 report on the Versailles Congress rooms recounts that the 1875 constitutional law's article 9 ("le siège du pouvoir exécutif et des deux chambres est à Versailles") was abrogated in 1879 precisely so the seat would no longer be constitutionally fixed ([Assemblée nationale, rapport n° 2226](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/12/rapports/r2226.asp) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). This matters for the cross-check: there is no rival legal capital designation floating around French law, and the only recurring non-Paris location for state ceremony is the Parliamentary Congress, which still meets in Versailles' Salle du Congrès ([Château de Versailles](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/decouvrir/histoire/les-grandes-dates/les-parlementaires-versailles) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)).

**The plausible alternative rivers a careful analyst must explicitly rule out are the Marne, the Oise, the (culverted) Bièvre, and the artificial canal network — and each fails for a documented reason.** The Marne and Oise are major *affluents* of the Seine — the Ministry of the Interior lists "l'Aube, la Marne, l'Yonne et l'Oise" as the Seine's principal tributaries ([Ministère de l'Intérieur](https://formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr/fiches-par-thematiques/histoire-geographie-et-culture/atlas-de-la-france/les-fleuves-fran%C3%A7ais/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)) — and neither is the river *of* Paris: the Marne joins the Seine at the city's south-eastern edge (Charenton-le-Pont/Alfortville/Ivry) and the Oise only joins some 20 km north-west of the city at Conflans-Sainte-Honorine ([Agence de l'eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/agence-de-leau/le-bassin-de-la-seine) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)); UNESCO's formulation is definitive on this point — Paris sits *between* the two confluences, on the Seine itself ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/fr/list/600) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The Bièvre is the genuine "lost second river": 36 km long, it entered Paris by the Poterne des Peupliers (13th arr.), ran about 5 km through the 13th and 5th arrondissements and joined the Seine at the Gare d'Austerlitz ([Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/pages/les-secrets-enfouis-de-la-bievre-riviere-parisienne-16900) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). APUR, Paris's public-interest planning agency, notes it was "le seul affluent de la Seine à avoir disposé d'un confluent naturel au cœur de Paris" — and also that its polluted waters no longer even reach the Seine, being diverted into the sewer system to the Achères treatment plant ([APUR, "La Bièvre", 2001](https://www.apur.org/sites/default/files/documents/40.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The inter-municipal Bièvre syndicate confirms that since 1912 the river has not flowed openly in Paris at all, lost its legal status as a watercourse, and only regained it in 2007, with partial re-daylighting in the southern suburbs (L'Haÿ-les-Roses 2016, Arcueil/Gentilly from 2021) ([SMBVB](https://www.smbvb.fr/la-bievre/historique/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)); the BnF's Gallica historical dossier and National Geographic France document the same trajectory, including ongoing Paris efforts to bring a branch back to the surface ([BnF/Gallica](https://gallica.bnf.fr/accueil/fr/html/lhistoire-tourmentee-dune-riviere-quand-la-bievre-disparait) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established); [National Geographic France](https://www.nationalgeographic.fr/environnement/des-parisiens-cherchent-a-faire-resurgir-une-riviere-enfouie-au-debut-du-20e-siecle) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established)). So "the Bièvre" can only be correct in a historical sense; since 1912 there is no open Bièvre in Paris. The canals — Canal de l'Ourcq (97 km to the Bassin de la Villette, built by a law of 1802 under Bonaparte), Canal Saint-Martin (4.5 km, half of it underground, 9 locks, linking the Villette basin to the upstream Seine at the Bassin de l'Arsenal), and Canal Saint-Denis (6.6 km, Villette to downstream Seine) — are 19th-century *artificial* waterworks, a 130 km network owned and maintained by the Ville de Paris itself, which by design "permettent à la navigation de « couper » une boucle de la Seine" ([Ville de Paris, "Les canaux de Paris"](https://www.paris.fr/pages/tmp-canaux-7834) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Ville de Paris, "Les canaux parisiens célèbrent 200 ans d'histoire"](https://www.paris.fr/dossiers/les-canaux-parisiens-celebrent-200-ans-d-histoire-50) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [VNF](https://www.vnf.fr/vnf/points-d-interetss/le-bassin-de-la-villette-a-paris/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). Someone answering "Canal Saint-Martin" is in fact naming an artificial feeder that ultimately discharges into the Seine. Finally, peer-reviewed historical geography (Noizet & Gautier, *Villes et rivières de France*, CNRS Éditions) documents further vanished natural watercourses — the Petite Seine arm near Saint-Germain-des-Prés, the Île Louviers side-channel infilled in 1844, and the northern palaeo-meander of the Seine whose buried course became the Grand Égout and still shapes right-bank street patterns — none of which survives as a surface watercourse today ([Noizet & Gautier 2019, HAL](https://hal.science/hal-04014374/file/PDF%20Auteurs_Paris.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Noizet, Robert & Mirlou 2011, Revue du Nord, HAL](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-00668371/document) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). Taken together, these sources establish why "the Seine" is the only correct *principal* answer: it is the sole natural river with an open channel crossing Paris proper (13 km, 37 bridges).

**Competing "capital" claims that a careful report must handle divide into (a) the two genuine historical interruptions — Versailles and Vichy — and (b) wartime seats of government that never displaced Paris's legal capital status.** For Versailles, the official Château portal is unusually candid: Louis XIV installed the Court and the seat of government there on 6 May 1682 and the city "devient le nouveau centre du royaume jusqu'en 1789, **sans que Paris ne perde son titre de capitale**" ([Château de Versailles](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/decouvrir/histoire/grandes-dates/versailles-capitale-royaume) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)); its teaching dossier is blunter still, calling Versailles "la capitale politique de la France" from 1682 ([Château de Versailles, ressource pédagogique (PDF)](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/sites/default/files/presse/documents/ressource-pedagogique_fiche_thematique_-la_construction_du_chateau_0.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary)). The second Versailles episode is fully documented in statute: after the 1870 defeat and the Commune, the Assemblée sat at Versailles from March 1871, article 9 of the 1875 constitutional law fixed "le siège du pouvoir exécutif et des deux chambres" there, and the ordinary **loi du 22 juillet 1879 relative au siège du Pouvoir exécutif et des Chambres à Paris** restored everything to Paris from 3 November 1879 ([Légifrance, loi du 22 juillet 1879](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000000521262) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Sénat, "1875-1940 : Le Sénat républicain"](https://www.senat.fr/connaitre-le-senat/lhistoire-du-senat/1875-1940-le-senat-republicain.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Assemblée nationale, rapport n° 2226](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/12/rapports/r2226.asp) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). Gambetta's 1879 formula — returning "à notre incomparable capitale le titre légal dont on l'avait trop longtemps dépouillée" — is the cleanest statement that Versailles held only the physical seat, not the title ([Assemblée nationale, rapport n° 2226](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/12/rapports/r2226.asp) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The wartime seats follow the same pattern: Tours then Bordeaux in 1870–71, Bordeaux from 2 September to 8 December 1914 (confirmed by the Senate's own history and the Conseil d'État's, whose institution "accompagne le Gouvernement dans son exil bordelais"), with Paris never formally demoted — a 1914 newspaper quoted in the BnF's Retronews says Paris remained "la capitale qu'il n'a pas cessé d'être en droit" ([Sénat, Bordeaux](https://www.senat.fr/connaitre-le-senat/lhistoire-du-senat/dossiers-dhistoire/la-grande-guerre-vue-des-commissions-du-senat/bordeaux.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Conseil d'État](https://www.conseil-etat.fr/actualites/le-conseil-d-etat-pendant-la-premiere-guerre-mondiale) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Retronews/BnF](https://www.retronews.fr/conflits-et-relations-internationales/long-format/2018/06/21/1914-le-gouvernement-sinstalle-bordeaux) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established); regional press counts "Bordeaux, trois fois capitale de la France" — 1870, 1914, 1940 ([Ouest-France / Passion Aquitaine](https://passion-aquitaine.ouest-france.fr/bordeaux-trois-fois-capitale-france/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established)); the public educational platform Lumni repeats the same ([Lumni](https://www.lumni.fr/video/est-il-vrai-que-bordeaux-a-ete-la-capitale-de-la-france) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary)). Vichy is the trickiest competing claim. The Allier departmental archives say the Pétain government "choisit Vichy comme capitale" from July 1940 for its hotel capacity and modern telephone exchange, a stay of four years ([Archives départementales de l'Allier](https://archives.allier.fr/service-educatif/expositions-virtuelles/lallier-durant-la-seconde-guerre-mondiale/-la-mise-en-place-du-nouveau-regime) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)), and historian Laurent Joly's scholarly chapter likewise describes Pétain fixing "la capitale de l'État français" at Vichy, with administrative gravity drifting back to Paris after November 1942 ([Joly, OpenEdition](https://doi.org/10.58079/w8wz) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established — abstract only consulted)). The Ministry of the Interior's civic-education sheet and Larousse, by contrast, describe Vichy neutrally as the *seat* of the government of the "État français" (1940–1944) ([Ministère de l'Intérieur, régime de Vichy](https://formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr/fiches-par-journ%C3%A9e/journ%C3%A9e-2/histoire/les-regimes-politiques-depuis-1789/le-regime-de-vichy-1940-1944/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Larousse](https://www.larousse.fr/dictionnaires/college/gouvernement_de_Vichy/42135) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established)), and the USHMM encyclopedia similarly says only that the collaborationist government "dont le siège se trouvait à Vichy" administered unoccupied France ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/fr/article/france) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established)). The defensible synthesis — and the one that matters for a "capital of France" question — is that Vichy was the de facto seat of government of a regime whose legality the Republic itself repudiated, while Paris remained the legal capital; after the Liberation the provisional government returned to Paris, "succédant au gouvernement de Vichy" ([Larousse](https://www.larousse.fr/dictionnaires/college/gouvernement_de_Vichy/42135) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established)). The counter-government of Free France adds a third candidate: the Fondation Charles de Gaulle states de Gaulle made Brazzaville "capitale de la France libre" from late October 1940 until he moved to Algiers in June 1943 ([Fondation Charles de Gaulle](https://www.charles-de-gaulle.org/les-activites/grand-public/expositions/de-gaulle-lamitie-franco-africaine-70eme-anniversaire-de-lafrique-francaise-libre-2/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established)), a framing repeated in the education network Canopé's teaching dossier and L'Histoire magazine ([Réseau Canopé (PDF)](https://www.reseau-canope.fr/fileadmin/user_upload/Projets/AFRICA_2020/Africa_FichePeda_Alger_et_Brazzaville_BAT.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary); [L'Histoire](https://www.lhistoire.fr/lafrique-capitale-de-la-france-libre) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established)). None of these rival cities displaces Paris today, and every consulted institution treats the episodes as interruptions of a continuing Paris capital.

**Two modern "confusion sources" the report should pre-empt are the "capitales régionales" vocabulary and the Strasbourg/EU question.** French officials and press habitually call regional prefectures "capitales régionales" — the Prime Minister's strategist body used exactly this term when analysing the 2015 regional reform's choice of chef-lieux (and notes Strasbourg was "désignée comme capitale régionale par la loi de 16 janvier 2015") ([Stratégie-plan.gouv.fr](https://www.strategie-plan.gouv.fr/choisir-les-capitales-regionales-entre-rayonnement-economique-et-localisation-geographique) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); the Documentation Française series does the same for Bordeaux and Toulouse ([Vie-publique.fr, rapport (PDF)](https://www.vie-publique.fr/files/rapport/pdf/984001116.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary); [franceinfo](https://www.franceinfo.fr/politique/reforme-territoriale/reforme-territoriale-decouvrez-la-carte-des-prefectures-des-13-nouvelles-regions_1022861.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established)). This is a sub-national administrative hierarchy — Larousse already notes Paris is simultaneously "capitale de la France" and "chef-lieu de la Région Île-de-France" — and creates no competitor for the national capital. Strasbourg attracts the other kind of confusion: it has been the seat of the Council of Europe since 1949 (article 11 of the Statute of London) and of the European Parliament by the 12 December 1992 Edinburgh decision, a clause replicated verbatim in Protocol No. 6 annexed to the treaties since Amsterdam — hence a Senate reply calling its "statut incontestable de ville européenne" and an Assemblée report christening it "Strasbourg, capitale parlementaire de l'Europe" ([Sénat, réponse ministérielle 2002](https://www.senat.fr/questions/base/2002/qSEQ020902697.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Sénat, rapport Vinçon n° 254 (1993–94)](https://www.senat.fr/rap/r93-254/r93-254_mono.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Assemblée nationale, rapport n° 2035 (2019) (PDF)](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/15/rapports/due/l15b2035_rapport-information.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The Council of Europe's own briefing confirms Strasbourg hosts the CoE, the ECHR, the (EU) European Parliament and the European Ombudsman ([Council of Europe (PDF)](https://rm.coe.int/1680488fcd) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). None of it makes Strasbourg the capital of France — it is the seat of European (and pan-European) institutions *in* France, while the French government, presidency (Élysée), premiership (Matignon) and both chambers remain in Paris. A related myth worth one sentence of correction is Lyon's "capitale des Gaules": historian Patrice Faure on France Culture states "contrairement à un mythe tenace, Lyon n'est pas la 'capitale des Gaules', mais seulement la capitale de la Gaule lyonnaise", and the Lyon municipal historians quoted in Lyon Capitale (citing Chopelin & Souriac, *Nouvelle Histoire de Lyon*, Privat 2019) go further — "aucun document antique ne la qualifie ainsi" and "Lugdunum ne fut aucunement aux Gaules ce que Paris fut et demeure à la France" ([France Culture](https://www.radiofrance.fr/franceculture/podcasts/le-cours-de-l-histoire/incontournable-lugdunum-lyon-a-la-confluence-des-gaules-2402376) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established); [Lyon Capitale / Archives municipales de Lyon](https://www.lyoncapitale.fr/actualite/dans-les-archives-lyon-capitale-des-gaules) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established)). Since a state research institute (Inrap) still loosely uses "Caput Galliarum" ([Inrap](https://www.inrap.fr/occupations-habitats-logements-pendant-l-antiquite-gallo-romaine-10247) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary)), I flag this as a soft conflict between institutional shorthand and scholarly consensus; either way, Roman Lugdunum is not a candidate for the capital of France, which did not exist as such in antiquity.

**Documented common factual errors on this axis are mostly adjacent to the answer rather than to the answer itself.** The most attested is confusing the Seine's *fame* with *length*: "Donner la Seine comme plus long fleuve : c'est la Loire" is listed among "erreurs fréquentes" by geography revision sites, and civics-exam prep sites report candidates routinely mis-picking "la Seine" on that multiple-choice question precisely "parce qu'elle traverse Paris" ([Feuille.org](https://feuille.org/fiches/geographie/fleuves-de-france/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Low, tier: Low — pop-education site); [Prépa-examen.fr](https://www.prepa-examen.fr/question/long-fleuve-france-csp/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Low, tier: Low — exam-prep blog)). The underlying fact is solid and official: the Loire is "le plus long fleuve de France" at ~1,006–1,020 km versus the Seine's ~776 ([Ministère de l'Intérieur](https://formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr/fiches-par-thematiques/histoire-geographie-et-culture/atlas-de-la-france/les-fleuves-fran%C3%A7ais/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The same sources document a second error — "croire que la Seine prend sa source à Paris ou en Normandie" (Low-tier, as above); in fact it rises at Source-Seine in Côte-d'Or, a site so often oversimplified that the City of Paris's own heritage portal warns the schoolroom version (Langres plateau, 471 m) is "pas faux, mais pas tout à fait vrai", since Paris has owned a multi-spring valley there since 1864 and the celebrated spring in a grotto with Jouffroy's nymph statue is a Second-Empire landscaped interpretation of one of several sources ([Patrimoine professionnel, Ville de Paris](https://patpro.paris.fr/lieux/les-sources-de-la-seine-un-parc-parisien-en-pleine-campagne) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Ville de Paris, exposition "Aux sources de la Seine" (PDF)](https://cdn.paris.fr/paris/2024/12/12/panneaux-exposources-de-la-seine_v9-light-WIg2.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)); altitudes given for the source range from 446 to 472 m across sources ([Sandre, via archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20210123171121/http://www.sandre.eaufrance.fr/geo/CoursEau/----0010) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary); [AESN](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/agence-de-leau/le-bassin-de-la-seine) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary)). Terminological errors recur too: several popular sources stress the fleuve/rivière distinction — the Seine is a fleuve (flows to the sea), the Marne, Bièvre and Ourcq are rivières, the canals artificial — and note the "five rivers" civic lesson is itself a simplification ([Ministère de l'Intérieur](https://formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr/fiches-par-thematiques/histoire-geographie-et-culture/atlas-de-la-france/les-fleuves-fran%C3%A7ais/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Geogir](https://geogir.fr/fleuves-france/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Low, tier: Low — self-declared AI-assisted content farm; consulted only for its source-by-source length comparison table, which matches the primary sources I checked independently)). Finally, publication-specific slip risks exist inside authoritative sources themselves: dates for the Bièvre's disappearance spread from "canalisée à partir de 1828… enterrée en 1912" (Ville de Paris) to "1875 chantier, achevé 1912" (CNEWS, **low tier**: [CNEWS](https://www.cnews.fr/culture/2014-02-23/pourquoi-la-bievre-est-elle-enterree-paris-659677) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Low, tier: Low)) to "dernier tronçon couvert en 1935" (APUR), and the Brazzaville capital date shifts between 28 August and 26 October 1940 depending on whether one counts the territory's rallying or de Gaulle's arrival ([Fondation Charles de Gaulle](https://www.charles-de-gaulle.org/les-activites/grand-public/expositions/de-gaulle-lamitie-franco-africaine-70eme-anniversaire-de-lafrique-francaise-libre-2/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established)). None of these disputes touches the capital–river pair itself. As an adjacent finding, note that France has debated physically moving parts of the state out of Paris for a decade — 3,900+ central-administration posts relocated to the regions by end-2022 under CAP 2022 ([modernisation.gouv.fr (PDF)](https://www.modernisation.gouv.fr/files/fileadmin-legacy/Book/Fiche4_13.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary)), with stalled Interior-ministry relocations challenged in the Senate as recently as 20 November 2025 (transcript reproduced by the data aggregator Pappers — **low tier as a source**; treat the quoted debate as Senate official record) ([Pappers, Sénat QOR 2025](https://politique.pappers.fr/question/decentralisation-services-ministere-linterieur-QSN477293) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Low, tier: Low)) — but no credible actor proposes relocating the capital itself, which indirectly reinforces that "Paris" is the stable, uncontested term of the answer.
