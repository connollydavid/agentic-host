# Research notes — axis: Capital status of Paris
# Parent topic: Capital of France and the river that runs through it
# Date: 2026-11-28

## Capital status of Paris

**The direct answer: yes, Paris is the capital of France, but its capital status rests on ordinary legislation, custom, and the concentration of state institutions there — not on any constitutional provision.** No French constitution since 1791 names a capital. As Giacomo Delledonne (Scuola Normale Superiore) states in a peer-reviewed analysis, "the Constitution of 4 October 1958 does not make reference to Paris as the capital of the French Republic," a silence that is all the more striking because Article 2 enumerates the Republic's other identity symbols — language, flag, anthem, motto — but conspicuously omits the capital ([Delledonne, Perspectives on Federalism](https://www.iris.sssup.it/retrieve/4982be06-23b0-4825-b7f8-e00df0b2256a/PoF_2024_Vol16-3_Symposium_3-Delledonne.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). This silence is corroborated independently by a peer-reviewed Polish comparative study, which notes that matters related to the seat of state authorities "are not subject to the regulation" of the 1958 Constitution ([Grabowski, Przegląd Prawa Konstytucyjnego](https://bibliotekanauki.pl/articles/941122.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)), and by a 2007 study of the Council of Europe's Congress of Local and Regional Authorities, which lists France among states where "no particular references to the capital city are to be found in the national constitutions" and classifies "Paris in France" as one of several "conventional capital cities without any legal recognition" ([Council of Europe CPL(14)4REP](https://rm.coe.int/statut-des-villes-capitales/168071ab77) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). As a secondary check (not a citable source), I full-text-searched a public transcription of the consolidated constitutional text currently in force and found zero occurrences of "Paris," "Versailles," or "siège," consistent with these findings and with the official text on [Légifrance](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/id/LEGITEXT000006071194) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary). France Culture, interviewing geographer Antoine Laporte, puts it popularly: "aucun texte de loi ni aucun paragraphe de la Constitution ne stipule que la capitale doit être Paris" — it is an evidence-based "évidence" of national tradition ([France Culture](https://www.radiofrance.fr/franceculture/jerusalem-brasilia-ankara-berlin-quatre-choses-a-savoir-sur-les-capitales-7282921) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Established)).

**The closest things to a formal legal basis are ordinary legislative and regulatory texts rather than the Constitution.** The key statute is Article 1 of Ordinance No. 58-1100 of 17 November 1958 on the functioning of the parliamentary assemblies: "L'Assemblée nationale et le Sénat siègent à Paris" — the National Assembly and the Senate sit in Paris — with the rider that "lorsque les circonstances exigent le transfert du siège des pouvoirs publics dans une autre ville," the Government, in agreement with the assemblies' presidents, takes measures to let Parliament sit near the President and Government ([Légifrance, ord. 58-1100 art. 1](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/article_lc/LEGIARTI000006530034) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). Delledonne reads this design as deliberate: because Paris is a "constitutional unthought-of" (un impensé constitutionnel), the regulation of its status "is reserved for ordinary legislation." At the regulatory level, the French state's own official terminology list — the Arrêté of 4 November 1993 on the names of states and capitals, issued by the foreign and education ministers (Juppé and Bayrou) and still in force — annexes a list giving, for "France (la) / République française (la)," the entry "Capitale ou siège du gouvernement: Paris" ([Légifrance, arrêté 4 nov. 1993](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000000181509/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)), updated by the Commission d'enrichissement de la langue française's official recommendation published in the Journal officiel of 24 September 2008 and 21 April 2019, which likewise lists "France (la) — Paris" under "Capitale, sièges diplomatiques ou consulaires" ([Légifrance, recommandation 2008](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000019509867) — see also [Ministère de la Culture toponym list](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/Media/Thematiques/Langue-francaise-et-langues-de-France/Infolettres/JUILLET-2019/Liste-complete-des-toponymes) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). Notably, the 1993 arrêté's own drafting principles define a capital, where several cities have capital rank, as "le siège actuel du gouvernement et/ou des représentations étrangères" — i.e., the French state itself operationalizes "capital" as "seat of government." International officialdom converges: the European Union's official country page for France states "Capitale: Paris" ([European Union](https://european-union.europa.eu/principles-countries-history/eu-countries/france_fr) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)), and long-running U.S. government country profiles list "Capital—Paris" ([U.S. State Department Background Note, archived](https://2009-2017.state.gov/outofdate/bgn/france/85084.htm) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). (The CIA World Factbook, another traditional source confirming "Capital: Paris," was formally sunset in February 2026; only unofficial mirrors remain, which I rate tier: Low and did not rely on — [CIA](https://www.cia.gov/the-world-factbook/countries/france/) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary, notice of discontinuation only).) The authoritative Encyclopédie Universalis also gives Paris as France's capital ([Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/france-le-territoire-et-les-hommes-donnees-naturelles/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Established)).

**Paris's capital status was, in fact, explicitly constitutional at the very moment it was fixed in its modern form — and the fix was done by a plain statute.** The constitutional law of 25 February 1875 (Third Republic), in its Article 9, provided that "Le siège du pouvoir exécutif et des deux chambres est à Versailles" — a consequence of the 1871 Commune, which had driven the elected government to Versailles. This is confirmed verbatim on the official Conseil constitutionnel history page ([Conseil constitutionnel](https://www.conseil-constitutionnel.fr/les-constitutions-dans-l-histoire/constitution-de-1875-iiie-republique) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)) and on the Presidency's own site ([Élysée](https://www.elysee.fr/la-presidence/les-lois-constitutionnelles-de-1875) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary)). A constitutional law of 21 June 1879 repealed Article 9 (single article: "L'article 9 de la loi constitutionnelle du 25 février 1875 est abrogé"), and the ordinary Law of 22 July 1879 "relative au siège du pouvoir exécutif et des chambres à Paris" then fixed the seat of the executive and both chambers in Paris, assigning the two chambers the palaces they still occupy — the Palais Bourbon (National Assembly) and the Luxembourg Palace (Senate) — while keeping joint sessions of the two houses (the Assemblée nationale/Congrès) at Versailles ([Légifrance, loi 22 juill. 1879](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000000521262) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). A peer-reviewed study of the Congrès in the Revue française de droit constitutionnel confirms both the Versailles clause of the 1879 law and its survival in substance today: "le siège du Congrès du Parlement demeure Versailles" ([Tetang, RFDC](https://droit.cairn.info/revue-francaise-de-droit-constitutionnel-2018-1-page-151?lang=fr) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The legal doctrine, summarized in the Revue de droit public by Olivier Renaudie citing Simone Dreyfus's classic 1960 thesis on capital-city status, is that a capital "defined by custom, usage or texts... remains stable and unchanged until an express and legal decision transfers it definitively elsewhere," and that a temporary transfer of the public powers does not change the capital ([Renaudie, Revue de droit public](https://www.cairn.info/article.php?ID_ARTICLE=RDP_090_1465) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). Renaudie also traces the term itself to a royal ordinance of 25 May 1413 declaring the Paris parlement "la Cour capitale de notre Royaume" and requiring the presidents of the parlement to reside "en notre bonne ville de Paris." This suggests that the Fifth Republic's constitutional silence is less an oversight than a settled confidence: as Delledonne's first hypothesis puts it, Paris's role as hub is "so self-evident that no explicit entrenchment of its role is needed."

**As seat of government, Paris hosts essentially every apex institution of the French state.** The President of the Republic, who under Article 9 of the Constitution presides over the Council of Ministers, works from the Palais de l'Élysée in the 8th arrondissement; the Élysée's official site describes the palace as "ce siège du pouvoir exécutif," where the weekly Wednesday Conseil des ministres, the Defence and National Security Council, and other key bodies meet ([Élysée — instances de gouvernance](https://www.elysee.fr/la-presidence/les-instances-de-gouvernance) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Élysée — Conseil des ministres](https://www.elysee.fr/la-presidence/le-conseil-des-ministres) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary)). Government spokesperson and civic-education site Vie publique (run by La Documentation française) confirms the Conseil meets weekly "traditionnellement le mercredi, à l'Élysée" ([Vie publique](https://www.vie-publique.fr/fiches/19475-quest-ce-quun-conseil-des-ministres) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary)). Both parliamentary chambers sit in Paris by force of the 1958 ordinance and the 1879 law (above), and the prime minister's official residence and the weekly seat of government, Hôtel de Matignon (57 rue de Varenne), is likewise in Paris. The high courts are also Parisian: the Court of Cassation, the Council of State (at the Palais-Royal), and the Constitutional Council sit in the capital, as the State Department's institutional profile and CNES's geographic study both list ([U.S. State Department](https://2009-2017.state.gov/outofdate/bgn/france/85084.htm) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary); [CNES GeoImage](https://cnes.fr/geoimage/paris-ville-capitale-ville-mondiale) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The CNES (French space agency) satellite-imagery study of Paris — an unusually concrete institutional source — maps this "espace au cœur du pouvoir politique exécutif, législatif et judiciaire de la France": Élysée, Place Beauvau (Interior), Quai d'Orsay (Foreign Affairs), Hôtel de Brienne (Armed Forces), rue de Varenne (Matignon, Agriculture), the Assemblée nationale facing the Pont de la Concorde, the Conseil d'État and Conseil constitutionnel at the Palais-Royal, and the Cour de cassation in the Palais de Justice on the Île de la Cité ([CNES](https://cnes.fr/geoimage/paris-ville-capitale-ville-mondiale) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary)). The one major exception proves the rule in an instructive way: the Congress of Parliament — which under Article 89 must approve constitutional revisions by a three-fifths vote, and under Article 18 hears the President's message — convenes in the Musée's hemicycle of the south wing of the Palace of Versailles, not Paris, as confirmed by both Vie publique ([Vie publique — Qu'est-ce que le Congrès ?](https://www.vie-publique.fr/fiches/19595-quest-ce-que-le-congres) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)) and the National Assembly's official summary sheet n° 55 ([Assemblée nationale](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/synthese/fonctionnement-assemblee-nationale/travail-legislatif/la-revision-de-la-constitution) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary)).

**France's capital city is itself given a uniquely controlled administrative status, reflecting the historical mistrust between "le pouvoir" and the capital.** Vie publique describes Paris as a "ville-capitale" whose particular regime dates to distrust after the 1871 Commune: Paris did not elect its own mayor until the law of 15 December 1975 (first applied 1977), long after the 1884 general rule on mayoral elections, and police powers remained with a Prefecture of Police whose prefect is appointed by the Council of Ministers — an arrangement in force since the arrêté of 12 messidor Year VIII (1 July 1800). Since 1 January 2019 Paris is a single territorial collectivity exercising both commune and department competences ([Vie publique — Paris, collectivité territoriale spécifique](https://www.vie-publique.fr/fiches/20152-paris-collectivite-territoriale-specifique) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)). The City of Paris's own history portal frames the same point: "Jamais Paris n'a connu le régime communal de droit commun" — never has Paris enjoyed the ordinary communal regime ([Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/pages/paris-des-carolingiens-aux-revolutionnaires-22384) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary)). This indicates that "capital" in France functions not as a constitutional status of the city but as a dense layer of ordinary-law special regimes justified by the city's capital function.

**Historically, Paris's capital status long predates any written constitution, and temporary displacements of the government never dethroned it.** The Bibliothèque nationale de France's official educational portal recounts that with the Capetian accession (Hugues Capet, 987) and especially from the 12th century, the king fixed his court and central administration at Paris, and that Philippe Auguste (Louvre fortress, royal archives at the Palais de la Cité, sedentarization of the curia regis) effectively made Paris the capital ([BnF Essentiels](https://essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/histoire/moyen-age/99f01b1e-76a4-42f6-bfd0-1700e9ebbf99-ville-moyen-age/article/3835a75d-9077-4cd1-95a1-11d9446dcd33-lutece-la-capitale) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)); Delledonne concurs that Paris's emergence "since the age of the Capetian kings" went hand in hand with state formation. During crises the seat of government moved without the capital changing: the government relocated to Bordeaux in September 1914 (installed 2 September, returned to Paris 8 December) per the Ministry of Armed Forces' history site ([Chemins de mémoire](https://www.cheminsdememoire.gouv.fr/fr/revue/larmee-francaise-en-1914) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary)), and again in June 1940 — after a stop in Tours, the government sat in Bordeaux from 14 June until 28–29 June 1940, then moved on to Vichy — the third time Bordeaux served as "capitale provisoire," after 1870–71 and 1914 ([France 3 Nouvelle-Aquitaine](https://france3-regions.franceinfo.fr/nouvelle-aquitaine/gironde/bordeaux/exode-maree-humaine-1-800-000-refugies-deferle-bordeaux-juin-1940-1836838.html) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Established)). The 1871–1879 Versailles interlude — and its quiet reversal by a simple law in 1879 rather than a constitutional amendment after 1958 — is the clearest demonstration that in French practice the seat of powers is transferable by ordinary means while Paris's capital status endures; Grabowski lists Orléans, Troyes, Versailles, Bordeaux and Vichy among cities that have temporarily performed capital functions while "the capital position of Paris is not questioned and therefore does not require constitutional guarantees."

**Cross-axis note (outside the stated axis, relevant to the parent topic):** the CNES study underscores that the city organizing all of this is structured by the Seine — "la ville est organisée par l'axe de la Seine," whose two banks are joined by some forty bridges and whose two artificial islands (de la Cité and Saint-Louis) are the historic core (and site of the Palais de Justice complex, itself part of the capital's institutional furniture). It also records the 1910 Seine flood and the great reservoir system built upstream (Pannecière, Lac d'Orient, Der) to tame the river through the capital ([CNES](https://cnes.fr/geoimage/paris-ville-capitale-ville-mondiale) (accessed 2026-11-28, tier: Primary)). This matters to the overall topic because the capital's institutional concentration and the river's course through the city are physically intertwined on the Île de la Cité, where royal and judicial power sat from the 10th–14th centuries (BnF) onward.

> Conflicts noted: No substantive conflicts on the core fact. One apparent tension is terminological rather than factual: the Council of Europe study calls Paris a capital "without any legal recognition" (meaning no statutory proclamation of capital status), while French official terminology decrees (1993 arrêté, 2008/2019 recommendations) do list Paris as France's capital or seat of government — but the latter are non-binding nomenclature texts, so both sources agree that no law formally declares "Paris is the capital"; they agree in substance, and the 1875→1879 sequence shows the seat of powers once was, and deliberately no longer is, fixed at constitutional level. Also flag for downstream use: the CIA World Factbook (historically "Capital: Paris") was discontinued in February 2026; surviving "Factbook" sites are unofficial mirrors (tier: Low) and were not relied upon.
