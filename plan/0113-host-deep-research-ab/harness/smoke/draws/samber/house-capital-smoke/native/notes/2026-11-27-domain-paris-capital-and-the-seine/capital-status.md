## Capital of France — capital status

**The capital of France is Paris, and France's own official references state it in exactly those terms.** The state terminology database maintained under the Ministry of Culture lists, for the entry "France (la)", the field *Capitale*: "Paris [Parisien, -ienne, n.]", published in the *Journal officiel* of 24 September 2008 [FranceTerme](https://www.culture.fr/FranceTerme/terme/AFET263) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The same date and source underlie the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' tabular "Recommandation concernant les noms d'États, d'habitants, de capitales, de sièges diplomatiques ou consulaires", whose annex gives, on the France row, "Paris" as the capital [Légifrance — Recommandation (JORF 24/09/2008)](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000019509867) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The National Toponymy Commission (Commission nationale de toponymie, CNIG), whose list is described as the "référence commune au public et à l'administration" and was validated by the CNIG plenary council on 16 September 2025, likewise records "France (la) — PARIS" [CNIG/IGN, *Pays, territoires et villes du monde*, February 2025](https://cnig.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/liste_pays_territoires_et_villes_du_monde_fev_2025.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary; PDF downloaded and text-extracted). The Interior Ministry's civic-training module for foreign residents states the proposition flatly — "Paris est la capitale de la France. 2,2 millions d'habitants y vivent" [Ministère de l'Intérieur, *Valeurs et institutions de la République française*](https://www.bouches-du-rhone.gouv.fr/content/download/22931/139092/file/module%201_Valeurs-et-institutions.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary) — and the state tourism operator France.fr consistently writes "la capitale française" / "the national capital" [france.fr](https://www.france.fr/fr/destination/paris/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). International institutional references corroborate the same: the European Union's official member-state page for France reads "Capitale: Paris" [European Commission — France](https://european-union.europa.eu/principles-countries-history/eu-countries/france_fr) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established). Taken together, four independent state sources plus one intergovernmental one put the answer beyond doubt.

**What France's constitution does *not* do is name a capital.** Article 2 of the Constitution of 4 October 1958 enumerates the Republic's attributes — the national emblem, the anthem, the motto, and (since 1992) the language — without any reference to a capital city, and Title XII on territorial collectivities is equally silent [Sénat — Constitution du 4 octobre 1958, texte originel](https://www.senat.fr/connaitre-le-senat/evenements-et-manifestations-culturelles/les-revisions-de-la-constitution/constitution-du-4-octobre-1958-texte-originel.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Public-law scholarship is unanimous on this point: Olivier Renaudie notes in the *Revue du droit public* that "Paris est ignoré par le texte de la Constitution de 1958", and contrasts this with constitutions that do name their capitals (Spain art. 5 for Madrid, German Basic Law art. 22 for Berlin, Turkey, Sweden), concluding that "l'élaboration du statut de Paris est laissée à la discrétion du législateur" [Renaudie, *Revue du droit public* 2019/6](https://droit.cairn.info/revue-revue-du-droit-public-2019-6-page-1465?lang=fr) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established). Giacomo Delledonne goes further, holding that "the capital city is not mentioned in any of the constitutions enacted since 1791", which makes Paris a "constitutional unthought-of" whose status is reserved to ordinary legislation [Delledonne, *Perspectives on Federalism* 16(3), 2024](https://www.iris.sssup.it/bitstream/11382/579553/5/PoF_2024_Vol16-3_Symposium_3-Delledonne.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established). A comparative study of EU member states reaches the same conclusion, adding that the long list of towns that have performed capital functions — Orléans, Troyes, Versailles, Bordeaux, Vichy — has never made the capital position of Paris questionable, hence "does not require constitutional guarantees" [Grabowski, *Przegląd Prawa Konstytucyjnego* 2017](https://doi.org/10.15804/ppk.2017.06.09) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Established). The Council of Europe's Congress of Local and Regional Authorities classifies France among the states where "no particular references to the capital city are to be found in the national constitutions" and lists "Paris in France" among "conventional capital cities without any legal recognition" [Council of Europe, CPL(14)4REP, *Status of capital cities*, 2007](https://rm.coe.int/status-of-capital-cities/16807199af) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary).

**The binding legal basis is therefore ordinary legislation fixing the seat of public powers, not a constitutional clause.** The operative text is Article 1 of Ordonnance n° 58-1100 of 17 November 1958 on the functioning of parliamentary assemblies, in force since 18 November 1958: "L'Assemblée nationale et le Sénat siègent à Paris," with a second paragraph providing that where circumstances require transferring the seat of public powers to another town, the Government, in agreement with the presidents of the assemblies, takes the measures needed for Parliament to sit near the President and the Government [Assemblée nationale — Ordonnance 58-1100, Art. 1](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/16/divers/texte_reference/08_ordonnance_58-1100_du_17_novembre_1958.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Sénat — Règlement du Sénat, Textes relatifs](https://www.senat.fr/reglement/pouvoirs_publics67.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Article 2 of the same ordinance allocates the buildings: the Palais-Bourbon and Hôtel de Lassay to the National Assembly, the Palais du Luxembourg with the Petit Luxembourg to the Senate, and the Congress chamber plus other premises "sis au château de Versailles" to the two assemblies for parliamentary congresses. This 1958 framework descends directly from the settlement of 1879. The constitutional law of 25 February 1875 had provided in its Article 9 that "Le siège du pouvoir exécutif et des deux chambres est à Versailles" — the only time a French constitutional text fixed the seat of government, and it fixed it outside Paris [Élysée — Les lois constitutionnelles de 1875](https://www.elysee.fr/la-presidence/les-lois-constitutionnelles-de-1875) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Article 9 was abrogated by the constitutional law of 21 June 1879 (single article, quoted on the Élysée page above), after which, as the Senate's own rapport puts it, "le siège des pouvoirs publics n'étant plus déterminé par une loi constitutionnelle, il revient aux chambres de le fixer par une loi ordinaire" — which the ordinary law of 22 July 1879 did, allocating the Luxembourg to the Senate and the Bourbon to the Chamber of Deputies, with the transfer dated 3 November 1879 [Sénat, Rapport n° 314 (2004-2005)](https://www.senat.fr/rap/l04-314/l04-3141.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary; PDF downloaded and text-extracted). The Assemblée nationale's corresponding report summarises the constitutional consequence in a sentence: "Désormais, le siège des assemblées ne sera plus fixé par la Constitution. Une loi ordinaire du 22 juillet 1879 confirme le retour à Paris," while Article 3 of that law kept the Versailles premises available for the National Assembly's joint sessions [Assemblée nationale, Rapport n° 2226 (2005)](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/12/pdf/rapports/r2226.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary; PDF downloaded and text-extracted). In short: since 1879, Paris's status as seat of the executive and both chambers rests on statute and on a 1958 ordinance that is itself amendable by ordinary law — this suggests that the capital is legally a consequence of where the institutions sit, rather than the reason they sit there.

**Every principal national institution is seated in Paris.** The presidency is based at the Palais de l'Élysée, which the Élysée's own heritage account describes as becoming under the Third Republic "la résidence officielle du chef de l'État" and, from 1958 onward under de Gaulle, the nerve-centre of the Fifth Republic's institutions [Élysée — 300 ans d'histoire élyséenne](https://www.elysee.fr/exposition/evreux-300) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The head of government works from the Hôtel de Matignon: the government's information portal states that the Prime minister "réside officiellement à l'Hôtel de Matignon … où se situent son bureau et son cabinet," that the cabinet and the Secrétariat général du Gouvernement are located there, and that the hotel has been assigned to the head of government since 1935 [info.gouv.fr — Le Premier ministre](https://www.info.gouv.fr/le-premier-ministre) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [info.gouv.fr — Bienvenue à l'hôtel de Matignon](https://www.info.gouv.fr/patrimoine/bienvenue-a-lhotel-de-matignon) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The two parliamentary chambers sit at the Palais-Bourbon (seat of the National Assembly since its allocation to the Council of Five Hundred in 1795, re-occupied in 1879) [Assemblée nationale — Histoire du Palais-Bourbon](https://www2.assemblee-nationale.fr/decouvrir-l-assemblee/patrimoine/palais-bourbon-et-hotel-de-lassay/l-histoire-du-palais-bourbon-et-de-l-hotel-de-lassay) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary) and at the Palais du Luxembourg, "Siège de la seconde chambre depuis 1799" and reassigned to the Senate in 1879 [Sénat — L'Histoire du Palais du Luxembourg](https://www.senat.fr/connaitre-le-senat/patrimoine/lhistoire-du-palais-du-luxembourg.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Among the high jurisdictions, the Constitutional Council states that it "siège dans l'aile Montpensier du Palais-Royal (2 rue de Montpensier, 75001 Paris)" and has rendered all its decisions there since 1958, except for 17 sittings between November 1958 and February 1959, with no secondary premises [Conseil constitutionnel — FAQ](https://www.conseil-constitutionnel.fr/faq) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); the Council of State gives its address as "1, place du Palais-Royal, 75100 Paris cedex 01" [Conseil d'État — Contacts](https://www.conseil-etat.fr/vos-demarches/contacts-et-informations-pratiques) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); the Ministry of Justice's directory puts the Court of Cassation at 5 quai de l'Horloge (audiences at 6 boulevard du Palais), Paris 1er [Ministère de la Justice — Cour de cassation](https://www.justice.gouv.fr/annuaire/fiche/cour-de-cassation) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); and the Court of Auditors has occupied the Palais Cambon, 13 rue Cambon, Paris 1er, since 1912 [Cour des comptes — contacts](https://www.ccomptes.fr/fr/choisissez-linstitution-que-vous-souhaitez-contacter) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Cour des comptes — Le Palais Cambon](https://www.ccomptes.fr/fr/cour-des-comptes/histoire-et-patrimoine/le-palais-cambon) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary).

**One genuine qualification to "all institutions in Paris" is the Parliament meeting in Congress.** Under Article 2 of Ordonnance 58-1100 and Article 3 of the 1879 law, the joint sitting of the two assemblies (the Congress, which under Article 89 of the Constitution adopts constitutional revisions) meets in the chamber at the Palace of Versailles, a location the Senate's report describes as "une affectation perpétuée pour les grandes heures de la République" [Sénat, Rapport n° 314](https://www.senat.fr/rap/l04-314/l04-3141.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). This suggests that a residual "second seat" exists in French constitutional practice — Versailles for the sovereign constituent assembly, Paris for everything else — which is why the plain claim "all national institutions sit in Paris" needs this carve-out.

**Historical exceptions are real but consistently treated as temporary transfers of the seat, not changes of capital.** The most durable was Versailles: the public institution that runs the palace dates the installation of the court and the king — Versailles becoming "le siège du gouvernement" — to 6 May 1682 and states that the town "devient le nouveau centre du royaume jusqu'en 1789, sans que Paris ne perde son titre de capitale" [Château de Versailles — Versailles, « capitale » du royaume](https://www.chateauversailles.fr/decouvrir/histoire/grandes-dates/versailles-capitale-royaume) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); UNESCO's World Committee assessment similarly describes Versailles as the residence and seat of power from Louis XIV to Louis XVI, with power returning to Paris on 6 October 1789 [UNESCO — Palais et parc de Versailles](https://whc.unesco.org/fr/list/83/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). Wartime and crisis relocations followed a recognisable pattern documented by the parliament itself: during the Franco-Prussian war the elected Assembly met at the Grand Théâtre de Bordeaux from 12 February 1871, and, despite Louis Blanc's speech of 10 March 1871 demanding that the government say it "regarde Paris comme la capitale nécessaire de la France" and voting on the ground that Paris "est la seule capitale possible de notre pays", installed itself at Versailles on 20 March 1871 after the Commune began [Assemblée nationale — Louis Blanc (10 mars 1871)](https://www2.assemblee-nationale.fr/decouvrir-l-assemblee/histoire/grands-discours-parlementaires/louis-blanc-10-mars-1871) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Assemblée nationale — Histoire résumée du Palais-Bourbon](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/histoire/7cg.asp) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). In September 1914 the government and presidency left for Bordeaux, the parliamentary session being closed by decree on 3 September and both assemblies transferring their seat there — the Senate at the Apollo music-hall — before the return to Paris in early December 1914 [Sénat — Bordeaux (dossier "La Grande Guerre")](https://www.senat.fr/connaitre-le-senat/lhistoire-du-senat/dossiers-dhistoire/la-grande-guerre-vue-des-commissions-du-senat/bordeaux.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). In 1940 the Senate dossier traces the full itinerary: government leaving Paris on 10 June for Tours, then Bordeaux on 14 June, Clermont-Ferrand on 29 June, and Vichy from 1 July, the Luxembourg palace being last used on 10 May 1940 and subsequently occupied by the Luftwaffe's staff [Sénat — Le Sénat itinérant : de Paris à Vichy](https://www.senat.fr/connaitre-le-senat/lhistoire-du-senat/dossiers-dhistoire/d38/le-senat-entre-1940-1944-de-paris-a-vichy.html) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); the Élysée palace was closed from June 1940 and only re-opened to the presidency in January 1947 under Vincent Auriol, de Gaulle having preferred the rue Saint-Dominique war ministry and then Matignon [France Culture — L'Élysée : d'hôtel particulier à palais du pouvoir](https://www.radiofrance.fr/franceculture/l-elysee-d-hotel-particulier-a-palais-du-pouvoir-en-trois-cents-ans-d-histoire-1864430) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). Crucially, none of these episodes produced a text naming a new capital: the legal machinery instead contemplates moving the *siège* — precisely the mechanism later codified in Article 1 of the 1958 ordinance. Grabowski's list of towns that have performed capital functions (Orléans, Troyes, Versailles, Bordeaux, Vichy) is the closest scholarly inventory I found [Grabowski 2017](https://doi.org/10.15804/ppk.2017.06.09) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Medium, tier: Established).

*(Adjacent finding, outside the stated axis but relevant to it.)* Because the constitution is silent, the capital city's own legal regime is ordinary-law territory and has been rewritten repeatedly: since 1 January 2019 the commune and the department of Paris have been merged into a single "collectivité à statut particulier" under Article 72 of the Constitution, created by Law n° 2017-257 of 28 February 2017 and recodified in Article L2512-1 of the General Code of Local Authorities [Légifrance — CGCT art. L2512-1](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/id/LEGISCTA000034115677/2019-01-01) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary); [Légifrance — LOI n° 2017-257](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000034103927) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: High, tier: Primary). This matters for the axis because it confirms, in practice, Delledonne's point that the capital's position is "reserved for ordinary legislation" — and it means nothing in the capital's legal architecture is entrenched against a simple statute. It is worth noting that academic reform proposals have suggested closing the gap by amending Article 2 to state that "la capitale de la République française est Paris" [GRÉCI — Propositions](https://www.legreci.fr/proposition/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Low, tier: Low — single scholarly association's advocacy page, not a parliamentary text), and that no such provision has been adopted: I found no adopted French statute or constitutional revision designating Paris as "capitale" by name.

> Conflicts noted: (1) Framing, not facts — the Council of Europe report says Paris's status rests on convention with "no legal recognition", while French primary law (Art. 1 of the 1879 law, Art. 1 of Ordonnance 58-1100) legally fixes the seat of the executive and both chambers in Paris. The two are reconcilable only if "capital" is read strictly as a formal label: no French text uses the word *capitale* to designate Paris, though state terminology lists fix "Paris" in the *Capitale* field. (2) Dates of the 1870–71 Bordeaux episode differ across sources: a regional feature gives "9 décembre 1870 au 10 mars 1871" [Ouest-France / Passion Aquitaine](https://passion-aquitaine.ouest-france.fr/bordeaux-trois-fois-capitale-france/) (accessed 2026-11-27, confidence: Low, tier: Low — popularising regional outlet), whereas the Senate's dossier describes the 1914 transfer precisely and the Assembly's own history pages give Bordeaux from 12 February 1871 for the elected Assembly. Likely difference: conflation of the Government of National Defence's stay with the elected Assembly's session. Using the parliamentary (Assembly and Senate) dates. (3) The Interior Ministry module's figure of 2.2 million inhabitants for Paris was not cross-checked against INSEE, and may be dated; not material to the capital claim.
> Gaps: No sources found for (a) any French statute or constitutional provision that uses the word "capitale" to designate Paris (searched in both French and English; only terminology lists and toponymy references were found); (b) a primary legal text (decree or law) formally transferring the seat of public powers in 1914 or 1940 — the Senate dossier describes the transfers narratively, including the decree closing the 1914 session; (c) official French sources designating Brazzaville, Algiers or Bayeux as capital or seat of Free France / the provisional government (only Wikipedia-type accounts surfaced, which are not used as sources); (d) Encyclopædia Britannica, which returned HTTP 403, so no Established encyclopaedia was consulted directly — the EU institutional page, UNESCO and peer-reviewed public-law articles were used instead.
