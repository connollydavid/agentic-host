## The Seine — course and geography

**Verification of the core premise.** The river that runs through Paris is the Seine, and this is confirmed by multiple independent French state sources rather than by encyclopaedic convention alone. The Ministry of the Interior's official civic-education atlas of France lists the Seine among the country's five *fleuves* (rivers reaching the sea) and names the major cities it crosses as "Troyes, Paris, Rouen and Le Havre" [Ministère de l'Intérieur](https://formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr/fiches-par-thematiques/histoire-geographie-et-culture/atlas-de-la-france/les-fleuves-fran%C3%A7ais/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The Seine-Normandie Water Agency, the public body in charge of the river basin, states that the Seine "rises at Source-Seine in the Côte-d'Or on the Langres plateau" and reaches the English Channel 773 km later (the same agency gives 777 km elsewhere — see Length below), and that the river "crosses … the city of Paris, which it divides into two banks" [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/agence-de-leau/le-bassin-de-la-seine) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The City of Paris itself documents the river's course through the commune as 13 km, spanned by 37 bridges [Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/dossiers/la-seine-coeur-battant-de-paris-216) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). UNESCO's World Heritage documentation supplies the capital-city link directly: the inscription text describes Paris as "the capital city of one of the first great nation states of Europe", built "along a bend in the River Seine, between the confluence of the Marne and the Oise Rivers" [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/600/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Taken together, four institutional sources with no shared editorial chain agree, so the premise of the wider study — Paris is the capital of France and the Seine is its river — is established at high confidence.

**Source, direction of flow and mouth.** According to the Seine-Normandie Water Agency the Seine is "a lowland river that rises at 446 m above sea level on the Langres plateau, in the commune of Source-Seine (Côte d'Or)", and its course runs 777 kilometres before it reaches its estuary in Normandy [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/La-Seine-sous-l-effet-du-changement-climatique) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The commune lies in Burgundy, roughly 30–40 km northwest of Dijon [Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/pages/5-choses-a-savoir-sur-les-sources-de-la-seine-28197) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary). The direction of flow is consistent across encyclopaedic and scientific sources: Larousse's encyclopedia records that after the Yonne confluence at Montereau the Seine "resumes a S.E.–N.W. direction, enters the Brie region and reaches Melun" [Larousse](https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/riviere-lac/la_Seine/143800) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Established), Universalis Junior describes "a trajectory from the south-east to the north-west, crossing Champagne, draining much of the Paris Basin … and threading through Normandy as far as its estuary, close to Le Havre" [Universalis Junior](https://junior.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/seine) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Established), and a geomorphological study published by INRAP/HAL confirms that in the Île-de-France the Seine and its tributaries "head to the north-west, towards the Vexin-Mantois and then Normandy", terminating "in an open estuary on the Channel" [INRAP / HAL](https://inrap.hal.science/hal-05101560) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The mouth is the English Channel (la Manche) between Le Havre and Honfleur, a formulation used identically by the Ministry of the Interior, the Water Agency and the World Heritage documents cited above (confidence: High). A useful nuance from the peer-reviewed chapter in UNESCO's own book series is that the downstream boundary between "river" and "estuary" is itself contested: hydrologists place the estuarine limit at Poses, some 120 km upstream of Le Havre, because tidal fluctuation is felt that far inland, whereas the administrative maritime limit has historically moved between Rouen and Berville [UNESCO Books, chapter "The Seine, the River dedicated to Paris"](https://doi.org/10.54677/cgdx8656) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary).

**Length.** The length most frequently given by official French sources is 777 km: the Seine-Normandie Water Agency's climate page [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/La-Seine-sous-l-effet-du-changement-climatique) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary) and the City of Paris dossier, which frames the river as "13 km (of a total of 777 km in length between the Langres plateau and the Le Havre estuary)" [Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/dossiers/la-seine-coeur-battant-de-paris-216) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The same figure appears in a technical synopsis of the river published by the river-basin stakeholders' association IGF [Initiatives pour l'Avenir des Grands Fleuves](https://www.initiativesfleuves.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Fiches-synoptiques-SEINE.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Established), and the GIP Seine-Aval research group repeats a 777 km course [GIP Seine-Aval](https://www.seine-aval.fr/question/fiche-origine-eau/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary). Reference works and the national water register sit slightly lower: the Sandre national reference dataset (the authoritative French hydrographic register, from which encyclopaedia figures are generally derived) lists "La Seine — Length: 775 km" [Sandre / Portail eaufrance](https://web.archive.org/web/20210123171121/http:/www.sandre.eaufrance.fr/geo/CoursEau/----0010) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary — archived snapshot), the Ministry of the Interior gives 776 km [Ministère de l'Intérieur](https://formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr/fiches-par-thematiques/histoire-geographie-et-culture/atlas-de-la-france/les-fleuves-fran%C3%A7ais/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary), and both Larousse (776 km) and Universalis Junior (775 km) fall in the same band [Larousse](https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/riviere-lac/la_Seine/143800) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Established). The defensible statement is therefore that the Seine is approximately 775–777 km long, and any figure of "about 776 km" is safe. Two outliers should be noted: the Water Agency's own basin page says the river reaches the sea "773 kilometres" downstream of its source, an internal inconsistency within a single institution [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/agence-de-leau/le-bassin-de-la-seine) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary), and the European Commission's Joint Research Centre river-basin fact sheet gives 754 km [JRC, European Commission](https://water.jrc.ec.europa.eu/pdf/seine-fs.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Low, tier: Primary — figure taken from a search-engine extract; the PDF would not download, so it could not be read in full). The likely difference is methodological: the 754 km figure almost certainly reflects a pan-European river/estuary geodata convention (measuring to a different estuary head) rather than the French administrative kilometrage. This suggests the spread of ±23 km is a definitional artefact about where the river stops and the estuary begins, not a disagreement about the physical river.

**Rank among French rivers.** Sources disagree on where the Seine ranks nationally. Britannica's student encyclopedia calls it "France's second longest river after the Loire" [Britannica Kids](https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/Seine-River/276976) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Low, tier: Established — consulted only via a search-engine extract), while Universalis Junior calls it "the third longest French river" [Universalis Junior](https://junior.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/seine) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Low, tier: Established). The Ministry of the Interior's table explains the discrepancy without settling it: it gives the Loire at 1,020 km (explicitly "the longest river in France"), the Rhône at 812 km "of which 522 km in France", and the Seine at 776 km [Ministère de l'Intérieur](https://formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr/fiches-par-thematiques/histoire-geographie-et-culture/atlas-de-la-france/les-fleuves-fran%C3%A7ais/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). This implies the ranking turns entirely on whether the Rhône's total length (which places it ahead of the Seine) or only its French course (which places it behind) is counted; the claim "second longest" should be reported with that caveat.

**Navigability — the central question for a capital built on the river.** Yes, the Seine is navigable through Paris, and it is a regulated, working waterway rather than a decorative one. Voies navigables de France (VNF), the national public operator of inland waterways, runs the "Avis à la batellerie" navigation notice for the basin, which defines the "traversée de Paris" (Paris crossing) as a distinct itinerary running from the upstream ring-road bridge at kilometre point 165.200 to the downstream ring-road bridge at PK 177.950 — a navigable length of about 12.75 km inside the city — and sets binding maximum vessel dimensions by reach: 180 m by 11.50 m for cargo vessels between the upstream ring-road bridge and the Pont Sully, reduced to 125 m by 11.50 m between the Pont Sully and the Pont de Bir-Hakeim, with passenger and pleasure craft capped at 125 m by 11.50 m throughout [VNF, Avis à la batellerie n°1-2020](https://www.vnf.fr/vnf/app/uploads/2020/01/VNF_brochure_2020-08-BD.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Navigation through Paris is also lock-free today: a specialist dictionary of French waterways records that the historic Écluse de la Monnaie was disused and demolished in 1923, that the "Seine parisienne" reach now has zero locks and is classed as 13 km of navigable waterway between Paris-Bercy and Paris-Saint-Cloud, and that the smaller arm at the islands (the bras de la Monnaie) is reserved for upstream traffic while the main arm runs under an alternating one-way system [Projet Babel, Dictionnaire des canaux et rivières de France](https://projetbabel.org/fluvial/rica_seine-riviere.htm) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Low, tier: Low — a specialist enthusiast reference with no institutional editorial process; its kilometrage matches VNF's official notice and the City of Paris, but it should not be treated as authoritative on its own). The State's own river-development plan for the Seine, adopted by the Île-de-France regional environment directorate (DRIEAT), adds the operational picture: the Seine is a "natural commercial axis between the Norman maritime interface and the Paris basin", it is "not subject to navigability failure at low water because of its regulation into pooled reaches by navigation dams", and, apart from floods of decennial rarity or greater, "the Seine remains entirely navigable downstream of Paris and largely navigable upstream for freight", with more than half of its low-water flow sustained by the reservoir lakes of the Seine upstream of Paris [DRIEAT Île-de-France, Schéma d'aménagement fluvial de la Seine, May 2019](https://www.drieat.ile-de-france.developpement-durable.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/schema_d_amenagement_fluvial_de_la_seine_-_mai_2019_-_web.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). That same document quantifies what actually moves through the capital: 4 million tonnes of goods per year inside the Paris ring road in 2017, out of 21.2 Mt for Île-de-France and 29 Mt for the whole Seine valley, with construction materials accounting for 63% of valley freight [DRIEAT Île-de-France](https://www.drieat.ile-de-france.developpement-durable.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/schema_d_amenagement_fluvial_de_la_seine_-_mai_2019_-_web.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). At basin scale, VNF reports managing 1,400 km of navigable waterways in the Seine and lower-Loire territory, of which 450–540 km are large-dimension ("grand gabarit") waterways, and that the Seine basin carries between 40% and 50% of all French river freight — 19.56 Mt in 2022 [VNF, Bassin de la Seine et Loire aval](https://www.vnf.fr/vnf/regions/vnf-bassin-de-la-seine/) and [VNF press release, 20 March 2023](https://www.vnf.fr/vnf/app/uploads/2023/03/Communiqu%C3%A9-VNF-r%C3%A9sultats-fret-2022-bassin-de-la-Seine.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary).

The ocean connection matters for a capital whose river is also a sea approach. HAROPA PORT, the single state authority that since 1 June 2021 has merged the ports of Le Havre, Rouen and Paris, states that there are 120 km of navigation between Honfleur and Rouen and describes Rouen as a river-sea port midway along the axis [HAROPA Port, Accès maritimes de Rouen](https://accesmaritimesderouen.haropaport.com/fr) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary); a specialist maritime surveyor's note for CESAM gives Rouen's terminal reach as "120 km of navigable Seine" with access for ships up to 292 m long and 150,000 tonnes deadweight at a draft of 11.30–11.70 m, 3,500–4,000 ship calls a year, and notes that since the dredging programme the Le Havre–Rouen transit takes about six hours on a single tide [CESAM](https://www.cesam.org/bibliotheque/cl-surveys-x-cesam-conjoncture-relative-a-haropa-port.html) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). So the standard claim that the Seine is navigable by ocean-going vessels as far as Rouen, roughly 120 km from the sea, holds; note, however, that the physical limit for large river barges upstream of Rouen is about 5,000 tonnes because of the river's width, depth and bridge air draught, versus 18,000-tonne convoys on parts of the Rhine or Danube [DRIEAT Île-de-France](https://www.drieat.ile-de-france.developpement-durable.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/schema_d_amenagement_fluvial_de_la_seine_-_mai_2019_-_web.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). This suggests the Seine's geographic significance is as a *regional* artery that happens to serve a global city, not as a deep-water corridor.

**Tributaries and basin.** Three tributaries dominate, and French official sources agree on the names. The Water Agency identifies the Yonne, the Marne and the Oise as "the three principal tributaries of the Seine" and gives each a length and confluence point: the Yonne rises in the Morvan at 738 m and joins the Seine at Montereau-Fault-Yonne after 293 km; the Marne rises on the Langres plateau at Balesmes-sur-Marne and joins at Charenton-le-Pont/Alfortville/Ivry-sur-Seine after 525 km; the Oise rises in Belgium at 309 m in the Bois de Bourlers and joins on the right bank at Conflans-Sainte-Honorine after 330 km. It adds the Aube and the Loing upstream of the Paris region and the Eure and the Risle in Normandy [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/agence-de-leau/le-bassin-de-la-seine) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The Ministry of the Interior lists the main tributaries as the Aube, the Marne, the Yonne and the Oise [Ministère de l'Intérieur](https://formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr/fiches-par-thematiques/histoire-geographie-et-culture/atlas-de-la-france/les-fleuves-fran%C3%A7ais/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary), and the Water Agency's climate page adds the Norman tributaries the Epte, the Andelle, the Eure and the Risle, noting that the Marne joins the Seine at the very edge of Paris — geographically important, since UNESCO's definition of the World Heritage property is framed by the Marne and Oise confluences [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/La-Seine-sous-l-effet-du-changement-climatique) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary). Larousse makes the hydrological point that the Yonne is "more important than the Seine by its flow, its length and the extent of its basin" [Larousse](https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/riviere-lac/la_Seine/143800) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Established) — which is why the Seine's physical source is in Burgundy while much of its water arrives from the south and east. Basin size varies by source: 75,000 km² for the Seine's own catchment [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/agence-de-leau/le-bassin-de-la-seine) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary), 77,000 km² on the same agency's climate page, 78,650 km² in the encyclopaedias, 79,000 km² in the IGF synopsis [IGF (river-stakeholders association)](https://www.initiativesfleuves.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Fiches-synoptiques-SEINE.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Established) and "more than 80,000 km²" in the geomorphological literature [INRAP / HAL](https://inrap.hal.science/hal-05101560) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary). These must not be confused with the *administrative* Seine-Normandie water district, which covers 94,500 km², 28 départements, 8,016 communes and 18.9 million inhabitants because it also includes the independent coastal Norman rivers [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/agence-de-leau/le-bassin-de-la-seine) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Discharge at the mouth is given as 563 m³/s mean at Le Havre by IGF [IGF (river-stakeholders association)](https://www.initiativesfleuves.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Fiches-synoptiques-SEINE.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Established), against roughly 330 m³/s as the City of Paris describes normal conditions in the capital [Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/dossiers/la-seine-coeur-battant-de-paris-216) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary) — consistent, since the tributaries join in between.

**The UNESCO property: "Paris, Banks of the Seine".** The site is inscribed on the World Heritage List under reference 600 (dossier 600bis) with the title "Paris, Banks of the Seine" ("Paris, rives de la Seine"), on the basis of the 15th session of the World Heritage Committee in 1991 [UNESCO World Heritage Centre, Decision 15 COM XV](https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/3518/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary), and the Ministry of Culture's own list of French World Heritage properties repeats the 1991 date [Ministère de la Culture](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/thematiques/monuments-sites/monuments-historiques-sites-patrimoniaux/les-biens-francais-inscrits-sur-la-liste-du-patrimoine-mondial-de-l-unesco) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary). Its precise geography is unusually specific: "the property comprises bridges, quays and the banks of the Seine in the historic part of its course (between the Pont de Sully and the Pont d'Iéna) and the Ile de la Cité and the Ile St Louis" [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/600/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). That stretch is about 5–6 km of the river's 13 km intra-muros course, so only roughly a third of the Paris section is listed. The selection logic is explicitly historical: the boundary "is based on the age-old distinction between upstream and downstream. Upstream, the port and river transport; downstream, royal and aristocratic Paris. It is this latter section of the Seine, where functions of the capital city have developed, that was inscribed" [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/600/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). It was inscribed under cultural criteria (i), (ii) and (iv) [UNESCO World Heritage Centre, Decision 15 COM XV](https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/3518/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Two updates change the numbers people usually quote: the property today covers 538 ha with a 3,194 ha buffer zone and a "minor boundary modification inscribed year" of 2024 [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/600/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary), whereas the State Party's own earlier periodic reporting recorded 365 ha and no buffer zone at all, describing that absence as "a gap" [UNESCO World Heritage Centre, Periodic Reporting Cycle 3, Section II](https://whc.unesco.org/document/217020) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). Paris's local planning document closes the loop on dates and legal effect: the extension and buffer-zone creation were approved at the Committee's 46th session (New Delhi, 2024, decision 46 COM 8B.39), and the buffer zone was given legal force in France by prefectural order n° IDF-038-2025-02 of 17 February 2025, reproduced in the annexes of the Paris local urban plan [Ville de Paris, PLU annex Title VIII](https://regles-urbanisme.paris.fr/plu-bioclimatique/servlet/plugins/document/resource?id=100&id_attribute=93&working_content=true) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The management picture that comes with the designation is also relevant geographically: the State, directly or through its public establishments, owns the quays of the Seine as fluvial public domain, and since 2014 (Left Bank) and 2016 (Right Bank) the lower quays have been closed to motor traffic across nearly the whole property [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/600/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary).

**Beyond the stated axis — three findings that strengthen the overall topic.** First, the source of the river that runs through the capital is *owned by the capital*: the Paris city council voted to buy the valley of the Seine's sources on 6 April 1864, for 12,500 francs, under Baron Haussmann as prefect of the Seine, and built a landscaped park with an artificial grotto (inaugurated 1867) containing a nymph statue by François Jouffroy; the City of Paris still owns and maintains the site more than 160 years later, and in June 2026 launched a new archaeological research programme at the antique sanctuary of Source-Seine [Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/pages/5-choses-a-savoir-sur-les-sources-de-la-seine-28197) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary), a purchase independently corroborated by the Académie des Sciences, Arts et Belles-lettres de Dijon, which gives the parcel as 1 ha 73 a 2 ca [Académie de Dijon](https://www.academie-sabl-dijon.org/celebration/la-ville-de-paris-devient-proprietaire-des-sources-de-la-seine/) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Established). This matters because it turns the river into a continuous line of civic identity between the source in Burgundy and the city — the capital literally owns the beginning of its own river.

Second, the river's role is now shifting from transport and spectacle towards *swimming*, and the numbers are official. Free public bathing in the Seine opened in Paris in summer 2025 at three sites — the Bras Marie near the Pont de Sully, the Bras de Grenelle between the Pont de Grenelle and the Pont de Bir-Hakeim, and Bercy at the Passerelle Simone de Beauvoir — as the stated heritage of the Paris 2024 Games, which held the triathlon and open-water swimming events in the river [Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/pages/baignade-dans-la-seine-l-histoire-d-un-retour-aux-sources-24319) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The regional audit court's findings, deliberated in July 2025 and published in November 2025, give the hard frame: open-air bathing in the Seine had been banned in Paris since 1923 because of heavy river traffic and poor water quality from direct sewage discharges; the three sites were chosen in consultation with the State "given the constraints of navigation and quayside use"; the City's own share of the swimming plan cost a net €84.85 million of €134.60 million committed, with the heritage beaches themselves budgeted at €13.44 million capital and about €4.24 million a year to run, plus annual charges paid to VNF and HAROPA Port; and no bacteriological data yet permit a formal classification of the waters under the 2006 Bathing Water Directive, so preventive closures in rain or high flow are built into the system [Cour des comptes / CRC Île-de-France, IDR2025-51](https://www.ccomptes.fr/sites/default/files/2025-11/IDR2025-51_0.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The State's figure for the whole operation is "an investment of €1.4 billion, of which €700 million by the State through the Water Agency", with the plan reported to have removed 75% of the river's bacteriological pollution by the time of the Games [Préfecture de la région d'Île-de-France](https://www.prefectures-regions.gouv.fr/ile-de-france/Actualites/Se-baigner-dans-la-Seine-promesse-tenue-de-l-Etat) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary), corroborated by City of Paris press material and by franceinfo [Paris Presse](https://presse.paris.fr/communiques/9640) and [franceinfo](https://www.franceinfo.fr/france/ile-de-france/il-sera-possible-de-se-baigner-dans-la-seine-a-paris-a-partir-du-5-juillet_7247664.html) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Established).

Third, flood risk is the geographic fact that conditions everything else about building a capital on this river. The City of Paris records a maximum water height of 8.62 m at the Pont d'Austerlitz in 1910 — the largest modern flood — against a normal height of 1 to 2 m, and normal discharge of about 330 m³/s rising to 2,400 m³/s that year [Ville de Paris](https://www.paris.fr/dossiers/la-seine-coeur-battant-de-paris-216) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary); the four upstream reservoir lakes (Marne, Seine, Aube, Pannecière) were built precisely to flatten winter floods and support summer flows [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/La-Seine-sous-l-effet-du-changement-climatique) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: High, tier: Primary). The Water Agency also warns that the Seine's dilution capacity per inhabitant is 700 litres/day/inhabitant against 17,000 for the Rhône, and that basin flows could fall 10–30% in 2030–2060 [Agence de l'Eau Seine-Normandie](https://www.eau-seine-normandie.fr/La-Seine-sous-l-effet-du-changement-climatique) (accessed 2026-11-28, confidence: Medium, tier: Primary). This suggests that the geography most relevant to the capital's future is not the river's length but its low flow relative to the population it carries.

> Conflicts noted: (1) **Length.** 777 km (Water Agency climate page; City of Paris; GIP Seine-Aval; IGF) vs 776 km (Ministry of the Interior; Larousse) vs 775 km (Sandre national register; Universalis Junior) vs 773 km (Water Agency basin page — inconsistent with the same agency's own 777 km) vs 754 km (European Commission JRC fact sheet, outlier; PDF could not be retrieved and read in full, so its methodology could not be checked). Reporting "approximately 775–777 km" as the mainstream figure and treating 754 km as a different measurement convention.
