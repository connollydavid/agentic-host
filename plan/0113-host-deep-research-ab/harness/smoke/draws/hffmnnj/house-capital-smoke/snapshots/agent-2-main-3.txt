From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

This article is about the current constitution. For a more general article, see [List of constitutions of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_constitutions_of_France "List of constitutions of France").

Constitution of the French Republic

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/Constitution_sceau.jpg/250px-Constitution_sceau.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Constitution_sceau.jpg) Constitution of France's [Fifth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fifth_Republic "French Fifth Republic") (1958), sealed with the [Great Seal of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Seal_of_France "Great Seal of France").

Overview

Original title | *(in French) Constitution française du 4 octobre 1958*

Jurisdiction | [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France")

[Ratified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratification "Ratification") | [28 September 1958; 68 years ago (1958-09-28)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1958_French_constitutional_referendum "1958 French constitutional referendum")

[Date effective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_date "Effective date") | 4 October 1958; 68 years ago (1958-10-04)

[System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government "Government") | [Unitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_state "Unitary state") [semi-presidential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-presidential "Semi-presidential") [republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic "Republic")

Government structure

[Branches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_branch "Government branch") | Three (executive, legislative and judiciary)

[Chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_chamber "Legislative chamber") | [Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_France "Parliament of France") ([Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_\(France\) "Senate (France)") and [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_\(France\) "National Assembly (France)"))

[Executive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_government "Executive government") | [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_France "President of France")\-led [Council of Ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_France "Government of France") responsible to the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_\(France\) "National Assembly (France)"); [Prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_France "Prime Minister of France") as head of government

[Judiciary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary "Judiciary") | [High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Court_\(France\) "High Court (France)") is established for presidential Impeachment purposes; an extra-judicial body, the [Constitutional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Council_\(France\) "Constitutional Council (France)"), reviews the constitutionality of laws; no other part of the court system is referenced.

[Federalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalism "Federalism") | Unitary

[Electoral college](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_college "Electoral college") | No, but senate elections mandated to be indirect

Last amended | 2024

Supersedes | [French Constitution of 1946](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Constitution_of_1946 "French Constitution of 1946")

The current **Constitution of France** was adopted on 4 October 1958\. It is typically called the **Constitution of the Fifth Republic** *([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language"): la Constitution de la Cinquième République)*,[\[1\]](#cite_note-1) and it replaced the [Constitution of the Fourth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Fourth_Republic "Constitution of the Fourth Republic") of 1946, keeping its [preamble](#Preamble). The current Constitution regards the [separation of church and state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism_in_France "Secularism in France"), democracy, social welfare, and indivisibility as core principles of the French state.[\[2\]](#cite_note-Conseil-const-2)\[*[non-primary source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research#Primary,_secondary_and_tertiary_sources "Wikipedia:No original research")*\]

[Charles de Gaulle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle "Charles de Gaulle") was the main driving force in introducing the new constitution and inaugurating the [Fifth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fifth_Republic "French Fifth Republic"), while the text was drafted by [Michel Debré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Debré "Michel Debré"). The constitution was [adopted by referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1958_French_constitutional_referendum "1958 French constitutional referendum") on 28 September 1958\. Since then, the constitution has been [amended twenty-five times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_amendments_under_the_French_Fifth_Republic "Constitutional amendments under the French Fifth Republic"), [notably in 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_constitutional_law_of_23_July_2008 "French constitutional law of 23 July 2008") and most recently in 2024\.[\[3\]](#cite_note-3)

## Provisions

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Constitution_of_France&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Provisions")\]

### Preamble

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Constitution_of_France&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Preamble")\]

The preamble of the Constitution recalls both the *[Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen "Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen")* of 1789, as well as the [preamble of the Constitution of the Fourth Republic of 1946](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Constitution_of_27_October_1946#Preamble "French Constitution of 27 October 1946"), which created at the time new constitutional rights for French citizens, including social and economic rights.

The preamble of the Constitution also establishes [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France") as a [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laïcité "Laïcité") and [democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy "Democracy") country, deriving its [sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereignty "Sovereignty") from the people.

Since a constitutional revision in 2005, the preamble includes the ten articles of the [Charter for the Environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_for_the_Environment "Charter for the Environment").

### Government institutions and practices

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Constitution_of_France&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Government institutions and practices")\]

The French Constitution established a [semi-presidential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-presidential_system "Semi-presidential system") system of government, with two competing readings.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) In one reading, the executive branch has both a [president of the republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_France "President of France") and a [prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_France "Prime Minister of France"), as is commonly seen in [parliamentary systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_systems "Parliamentary systems") with a symbolic president and a prime minister who directs the government.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) This reading is supported by Articles 5 and 21 of the Constitution, which respectively state that the president is a guardian of the state and of the Constitution, while the prime minister has the power to decide on the government's actions and policies.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4)

In the other reading, the [parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Parliament "French Parliament") is very weak for a parliamentary system.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) The parliament has a limited legislative competence: article 34 of the Constitution lists domains exclusive to parliamentary legislation, but the remaining domains are left to the executive's regulations.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) The president also has the crucial powers to call a [referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Referendum "Referendum") and to dissolve the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_\(France\) "National Assembly (France)").[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) While the parliament may pass a vote of no confidence in the government, so that the government has to resign, this has been rare, happening in 1962 and on 4 December 2024\.[\[5\]](#cite_note-5)

Charles de Gaulle, the first president of the Fifth Republic, was instrumental in the adoption of the new constitution, as he was called back from retirement and narrowly avoided a coup resulting from the [Algerian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algerian_War "Algerian War").[\[6\]](#cite_note-:0-6)[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) De Gaulle always supported the second interpretation of the constitution, in favor of a powerful president.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) The first socialist president, François Mitterrand, elected in 1981, also supported this interpretation.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4)

Beginning in 1986, elections have from time to time resulted in parliaments with a majority that did not support the president.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) Such periods are known in France as [cohabitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohabitation_\(government\) "Cohabitation (government)"), where a president appoints a prime minister from the new parliamentary majority.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) During cohabitation, besides powers reserved to the president by the Constitution,[\[7\]](#cite_note-7) all other government powers would be exercised by the prime minister.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) In 2000, the Constitution was amended by shortening the president's term of office from seven years to five, to coincide with the term of parliament.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) The amendment means that the presidential election would take place around the parliamentary election, making it more likely to have winners who agree with one another and make cohabitation less likely.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4)

The Constitution provides for the election of the president and the [parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_France "Parliament of France"), the selection of the government, the powers of each and the relations between them.[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) It ensures judicial authority and creates a High Court (a never-as-yet-convened court for trying the government),[\[8\]](#cite_note-8) a [Constitutional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Council_\(France\) "Constitutional Council (France)") (an innovation of the Fifth Republic),[\[4\]](#cite_note-:1-4) and an Economic and Social Council.

### Shared lawmaking power

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Constitution_of_France&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Shared lawmaking power")\]

A unique feature of the Constitution of the Fifth Republic is that it establishes a shared law-making power between two branches of government: the legislative branch, where such powers resided in previous constitutions; and the executive branch, headed by the president and the prime minister appointed by the president.[\[9\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEElliottJeanpierreVernon200637-9)

Parliament has the fundamental responsibility for passing legislation in the Fifth Republic. There are two Houses of Parliament: the National Assembly and the [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_\(France\) "Senate (France)"). The Assembly is directly elected, and the more important, and has primary power in passing legislation; the Senate can delay legislation, but not ultimately block it.[\[10\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEElliottJeanpierreVernon200642-10)

Traditionally, the prime minister is the executive branch's liaison with the parliament; [Article 49](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_49_of_the_French_Constitution "Article 49 of the French Constitution") says they must pledge this role. This is weaker than the constitutions of the [Third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Third_French_Republic "Constitution of the Third French Republic") or [Fourth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Fourth_French_Republic "Constitution of the Fourth French Republic") republics, where the government could not be installed until the parliament had received the pledge from the prime minister.[\[11\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDicksonHübner19948–9-11)

The unique aspect in the Fifth Republic is in [Article 21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_21_of_the_French_Constitution?action=edit&redlink=1 "Article 21 of the French Constitution (page does not exist)") \[[fr](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article%2021%20de%20la%20Constitution%20de%20la%20Cinquième%20République%20française "fr:Article 21 de la Constitution de la Cinquième République française")\], where the prime minister has power to make legislation.[\[9\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEElliottJeanpierreVernon200637-9) In another unique feature in [Article 38](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_38_of_the_French_Constitution?action=edit&redlink=1 "Article 38 of the French Constitution (page does not exist)") \[[fr](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article%2038%20de%20la%20Constitution%20de%20la%20Cinquième%20République%20française "fr:Article 38 de la Constitution de la Cinquième République française")\], the Parliament can [temporarily delegate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_delegation_in_France?action=edit&redlink=1 "Legislative delegation in France (page does not exist)") \[[fr](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Délégation%20législative "fr:Délégation législative")\] a portion of its constitutional law-making power to the government to enable rapid consummation of urgent legislation, by passing an enabling law of legislative delegation called a *loi d'habilitation* ("enabling law").[\[11\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDicksonHübner19948–9-11)

According to Article 38, a *loi d'habilitation* may be issued by the parliament upon request of the government to temporarily delegate the parliament's constitutional law-making power to the government in a specifically defined subject area and for a specific length of time. While in effect, Parliament is blocked from issuing statutes in that area, and the government is permitted to draw up *ordonnances* that normally would be beyond their remit. The *ordonnance* comes into effect immediately, but must be ratified by Parliament before the end of the period or it expires. Until ratification, the *ordonnance* has the same status as a *réglement* (regulation), and can therefore be challenged by the [Council of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conseil_d'État "Conseil d'État"); but after ratification, it takes on the same status as a statute (*loi*), and can no longer be challenged. In practice, there have been 23 such *lois d'habilitation* from 1960 to 1990, with effective periods from one month to three and a half years, resulting in 150 *ordonnances*. About a third of them were subsequently ratified by Parliament. The *loi d'habilitation* is a new constitutional feature, not present in earlier constitutions.[\[11\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDicksonHübner19948–9-11)

[![Wikisource logo](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4c/Wikisource-logo.svg/40px-Wikisource-logo.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Wikisource-logo.svg)

French [Wikisource](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikisource "Wikisource") has original text related to this article:

**[Declaration of the Rights of Man](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/fr:Déclaration%20des%20Droits%20de%20l’Homme%20et%20du%20Citoyen%20de%201789 "s:fr:Déclaration des Droits de l’Homme et du Citoyen de 1789")**

Power sharing was unique in being part of the constitution in the Fifth Republic, but the practice was not recent.

The determination that the parliament has responsibility for the law goes back to article 6 of the [Declaration of the Rights of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man "Declaration of the Rights of Man") of 1789, and the role of the executive branch was only to execute it. In theory, the parliament would specify general laws, and the executive could only make regulations about how to apply the laws to day-to-day situations. In practice, this turned out differently, as the parliament on its own initiative sometimes passed acts delegating to the executive the right to alter or void acts of parliament, called *décrets-lois*. This practice slowly found its way into the Constitution.[\[12\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEElliottJeanpierreVernon200669-12)

Although [Article 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_1_of_the_Constitution_of_the_Fifth_French_Republic "Article 1 of the Constitution of the Fifth French Republic") of the [Third Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Third_Republic "French Third Republic")'s Constitutional Act of 25 February 1874\[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*\]\[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*\] explicitly forbade the parliament to delegate its responsibility, within five years this was ignored and had occurred several times. In 1939, in the run-up to the Second World War, Parliament gave the government power to enact decrees to protect the country. This practice became entrenched after the war, despite the fact that Article 13 of the [1946 Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Constitution_of_1946 "French Constitution of 1946") of the newly founded Fourth Republic expressly forbade it.[\[a\]](#cite_note-13) Part of the reason for this, was a lot of squabbling among numerous small political parties in the parliament, who were unable to agree on anything and were ineffective in passing legislation. This became especially problematic in the 1950s, as the [crisis in Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algerian_Crisis "Algerian Crisis") began to heat up, and the parliament was unable to deal with it. Charles de Gaulle, a private citizen at the time, conceived that the way out was to have a more powerful executive and a weaker parliament; when he was finally invited to form a new government in 1958 and write a constitution, his ideas were incorporated into the [Constitution of the Fifth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Fifth_Republic "Constitution of the Fifth Republic"), including the legislative power-sharing defined in Articles 21 and 38\.[\[13\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEElliottJeanpierreVernon200669–70-14)

In the original version of Article 37, everything that was not reserved to the legislative domain in the article was of a regulatory character (i.e., under control of the executive branch), although that clause was removed later. Since 1982, the legislative domain expanded, and since the landmark 1971 decision of the [Constitutional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Council_\(France\) "Constitutional Council (France)"),\[*[which?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Avoid_weasel_words "Wikipedia:Avoid weasel words")*\] additional sources were defined as part of the [constitutional block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_block_\(France\) "Constitutional block (France)"), such as the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and the 1946 constitutional preamble, which were henceforth part of the legislative domain, and after further reforms in 1996, the legislative domain has more power than was originally thought in 1958\.[\[14\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEElliottJeanpierreVernon200670–72-15)

### Treaties and the EU

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Constitution_of_France&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Treaties and the EU")\]

It enables the ratification of international treaties[\[15\]](#cite_note-16) and those associated with the European Union. It is unclear whether the wording, especially the reserves of reciprocity, is compatible with [European Union law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_law "European Union law").\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*\]

### Amendment

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Constitution_of_France&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Amendment")\]

The Constitution also sets out methods for its own amendment: either a [referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Referendum "Referendum") (article 11) or a parliamentary process with presidential consent. The normal procedure of constitutional amendment is that the amendment must be adopted in identical terms by both houses of parliament and then must be adopted by a simple majority in a referendum or by a three-fifths supermajority of the [French Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Congress "French Congress"), a joint session of both houses of Parliament (article 89).

### Judicial review

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Constitution_of_France&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Judicial review")\]

Prior to 1971, though executive, administrative and judicial decisions had to comply with the general principles of law ([jurisprudence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence "Jurisprudence") derived from law and the practice of law in general), there were no such restrictions on legislation. It was assumed that unelected judges and other appointees should not be able to overrule laws voted for by the directly elected French parliament.

Further information (in French): [Constitutional block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_block?action=edit&redlink=1 "Constitutional block (page does not exist)") \[[fr](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloc%20de%20constitutionnalité "fr:Bloc de constitutionnalité")\]

One of the cornerstones of the Constitution of the French Fifth Republic was the establishment of the [Constitutional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Council_\(France\) "Constitutional Council (France)"), comprising nine justices, who oversaw the constitutionality of legislation (treaties, statutes, regulations), ensured election and referendum oversight, and arbitrated legislative disputes between the President and National Assembly.[\[16\]](#cite_note-17) This followed a broader trend during post-war Europe to establish specialized judiciary tribunals to serve as a bulwark against unconstitutional legislative activities.[\[17\]](#cite_note-18) However, the Council was quite limited in its power under de Gaulle’s presidency and was only decisive when it erroneously upheld a popular referendum to streamline the popular presidential election via the Constitution.[\[18\]](#cite_note-19) Following [de Gaulle’s resignation in 1969](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1969_French_constitutional_referendum "1969 French constitutional referendum"), the Council entertained greater judicial power and discretion upon adjudicating in the consequential political crisis. The staunch Gaullist [Georges Pompidou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Pompidou "Georges Pompidou") was elected as de Gaulle’s replacement. He faced a political crisis when his Prime Minister [Jacques Chaban-Delmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Chaban-Delmas "Jacques Chaban-Delmas") pressured the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_\(France\) "National Assembly (France)") into banning the radical [Proletarian Left (La gauche prolétarienne)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauche_prolétarienne "Gauche prolétarienne") twice, which he deemed a threat to the public order and national security. This led to opposition from the [French Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_\(France\) "Senate (France)"), whose president appealed to the Constitutional Council.[\[19\]](#cite_note-20)

Consequently, in 1971, the Constitutional Council ruled its landmark decision [71-44 DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_71-44_DC_of_16_July_1971 "Decision 71-44 DC of 16 July 1971"), better known as the 1971 Freedom of Association Decision.[\[20\]](#cite_note-21) In such, the Council broke precedent by striking down legislation that allegedly violated the right to freedom of association, thereby fostering the "Constitutional Block."[\[21\]](#cite_note-22) The Block consisted of the 1958 Constitution, explicit standards ([Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen "Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen"), [the Preamble to the Constitution of the Fourth Republic or 1946 Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Constitution_of_27_October_1946 "French Constitution of 27 October 1946")), and implicit standards (the [fundamental principles of the Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_principles_of_the_Republic_\(France\) "Fundamental principles of the Republic (France)")—indivisibility, secularism, democracy, equal opportunity). Thus, according to the Council, the actions taken violated the collective principles of the Constitutional Block. Prior to the 1971 Freedom of Association Decision, the Council could only verify laws under the explicit textual stipulation of the 1958 Constitution.
