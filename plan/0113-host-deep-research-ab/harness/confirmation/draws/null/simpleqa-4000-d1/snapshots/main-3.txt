**Robert Hooke** ([/hʊk/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); 18 July 1635 – 3 March 1703)[\[4\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESinger1976116-5)[\[a\]](#cite_note-dual_dating-4) was an English [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath "Polymath") who was active as a physicist ('[natural philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy "Natural philosophy")'), astronomer, geologist, meteorologist, and architect.[\[5\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESinger1976115-6) He is credited as one of the first scientists to investigate living things at [microscopic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscopic_scale "Microscopic scale") in 1665,[\[6\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTE'Espinasse1956[httpsarchiveorgdetailsroberthooke0000margpage54mode2up_54]-7) using a [compound microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_microscope "Compound microscope") that he designed.[\[7\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGase2019-8) Hooke was an impoverished scientific inquirer in young adulthood who went on to become one of the most important scientists of his time.[\[8\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood20034,_5-9) After the [Great Fire of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Fire_of_London "Great Fire of London") in 1666, Hooke (as a surveyor and architect) attained wealth and esteem by performing more than half of the [property line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_\(real_estate\) "Boundary (real estate)") surveys and assisting with the city's rapid reconstruction.[\[9\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAubrey1898411-10)[\[8\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood20034,_5-9) Often vilified by writers in the centuries after his death, his reputation was restored at the end of the twentieth century and he has been called "England's [Leonardo \[da Vinci\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci "Leonardo da Vinci")".[\[10\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChapman1996-11)

Hooke was a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") and from 1662, he was its first Curator of Experiments.[\[9\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAubrey1898411-10) From 1665 to 1703, he was also [Professor of Geometry at Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_Professor_of_Geometry "Gresham Professor of Geometry").[\[11\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'ConnorRobertson2002-12) Hooke began his scientific career as an assistant to the physical scientist [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle"). Hooke built the [vacuum pumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_pump "Vacuum pump") that were used in Boyle's experiments on [gas law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_law "Gas law") and also conducted experiments.[\[12\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarsch2006867–9-13) In 1664, Hooke identified the rotations of [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars "Mars") and [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter "Jupiter").[\[11\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'ConnorRobertson2002-12) Hooke's 1665 book *[Micrographia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrographia "Micrographia")*, in which he coined the term *[cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_\(biology\) "Cell (biology)")*, encouraged microscopic investigations.[\[13\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGest2004-14)[\[14\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGest2009-15) Investigating [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics "Optics") – specifically light [refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction "Refraction") – Hooke inferred a [wave theory of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_theory_of_light "Wave theory of light").[\[15\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidson2015-16) His is the first-recorded hypothesis of the cause of the expansion of matter by heat,[\[16\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003257,_258-17) of air's composition by small particles in constant motion that thus generate its pressure,[\[17\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin2017140,_141-18) and of heat as energy.[\[18\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003299-19)

In physics, Hooke inferred that gravity obeys an [inverse square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_square_law "Inverse square law") and arguably was the first to hypothesise such a relation in planetary motion,[\[19\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAubrey1898413–415-20)[\[20\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin2017150-21) a principle [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") furthered and formalised in [Newton's law of universal gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation "Newton's law of universal gravitation").[\[21\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChisholm1911-22) [Priority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_priority "Scientific priority") over this insight contributed to the [rivalry between Hooke and Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton–Hooke_priority_controversy_for_the_inverse_square_law "Newton–Hooke priority controversy for the inverse square law"). In geology and [palaeontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleontology "Paleontology"), Hooke identified the organic origin and significance of fossils embedded in [sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock "Sedimentary rock"), thus questioning the Biblical view of the Earth's creation.[\[22\]](#cite_note-23) His explanations for the origin of such fossils, some of which he identified as being of extinct species, presaged 19th-century arguments for [biological evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution "Evolution").[\[23\]](#cite_note-24) He also argued that hills and mountains had become elevated by geological processes (rather than having been created exactly as they are today).[\[24\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin20173-25)

## Life and works

### Early life

Much of what is known of Hooke's early life comes from an autobiography he commenced in 1696 but never completed; [Richard Waller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Waller_\(d._1715\) "Richard Waller (d. 1715)") FRS mentions it in his introduction to *The Posthumous Works of Robert Hooke, M.D. S.R.S.*, which was printed in 1705\.[\[25\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaller1705i-26)[\[b\]](#cite_note-28) The work of Waller, along with [John Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ward_\(academic\) "John Ward (academic)")'s *Lives of the Gresham Professors*,[\[27\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWard1740169-29) and [John Aubrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Aubrey "John Aubrey")'s *Brief Lives*[\[28\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAubrey1898-30) form the major near-contemporaneous biographical accounts of his life.

Hooke was born in 1635 in [Freshwater, Isle of Wight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freshwater,_Isle_of_Wight "Freshwater, Isle of Wight"), to Cecily Gyles and the Anglican priest John Hooke, who was the curate of [All Saints' Church, Freshwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Saints'_Church,_Freshwater "All Saints' Church, Freshwater").[\[29\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJardine200323-31) Robert was the youngest, by seven years, of four siblings (two boys and two girls); he was frail and not expected to live.[\[30\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin2000-32)[\[31\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDrake199610-33) Although his father gave him some instruction in English, [(Latin) Grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Latin#Latin_in_school_education_1500–1700 "Neo-Latin"), and [Divinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity_\(academic_discipline\) "Divinity (academic discipline)"), Robert's education was largely neglected.[\[32\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaller1705ii-34) Left to his own devices, he made little mechanical toys; seeing a brass clock dismantled, he built a wooden replica that "would go".[\[32\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaller1705ii-34)

Hooke's father died in October 1648, leaving £40 in his will to Robert (plus another £10 held over from his grandmother).[\[33\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin20174-35)[\[c\]](#cite_note-39) At the age of 13, he took this to London to become an apprentice to the celebrated painter [Peter Lely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lely "Peter Lely").[\[35\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTENakajima1994-37) Hooke also had "some instruction in drawing" from the [limner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limner "Limner") Samuel Cowper[\[34\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAubrey1898410-36) but "the smell of the Oil Colours did not agree with his Constitution, increasing his Head-ache to which he was ever too much subject", and he became a pupil at [Westminster School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_School "Westminster School"), living with its master [Richard Busby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Busby "Richard Busby").[\[37\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaller1705iii-40) Hooke quickly mastered Latin, Greek, and [Euclid's *Elements*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_Elements "Euclid's Elements");[\[11\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'ConnorRobertson2002-12) he also learnt to play the [organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipe_organ "Pipe organ")[\[38\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPugliese2004-41) and began his lifelong study of mechanics.[\[11\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'ConnorRobertson2002-12) He remained an accomplished draughtsman, as he was later to demonstrate in his drawings that illustrate the work of [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle") and Hooke's own *Micrographia*.[\[39\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJardine200387,_88-42)

### Oxford

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a3/The_Shannon_Portrait_of_the_Hon_Robert_Boyle.jpg/250px-The_Shannon_Portrait_of_the_Hon_Robert_Boyle.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:The_Shannon_Portrait_of_the_Hon_Robert_Boyle.jpg)

1689 portrait of [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle") by [Johann Kerseboom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Kerseboom "Johann Kerseboom"), at [Gawthorpe Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gawthorpe_Hall "Gawthorpe Hall"), Lancashire

In 1653, Hooke secured a place at [Christ Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church,_Oxford "Christ Church, Oxford"), [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"), receiving free tuition and accommodation as an [organist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organist "Organist") and a [chorister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorister "Chorister"), and a basic income as a [servitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servitor "Servitor"),[\[40\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJardine200365-43)[\[d\]](#cite_note-45) (despite the fact he did not officially [matriculate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matriculate "Matriculate") until 1658\.[\[40\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJardine200365-43)) In 1662, Hooke was awarded a [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts_\(Oxford,_Cambridge,_and_Dublin\) "Master of Arts (Oxford, Cambridge, and Dublin)") degree.[\[38\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPugliese2004-41)

While a student at Oxford, Hooke was also employed as an assistant to Dr [Thomas Willis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Willis "Thomas Willis") – a physician, chemist, and member of the [Oxford Philosophical Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Philosophical_Club "Oxford Philosophical Club").[\[42\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin201710-46)[\[e\]](#cite_note-48) The Philosophical Club had been founded by [John Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilkins "John Wilkins"), Warden of [Wadham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadham_College "Wadham College"), who led this important group of scientists who went on to form the nucleus of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society").[\[44\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESyfret194878-49) In 1659, Hooke described to the club some elements of a method of heavier-than-air flight but concluded human muscles were insufficient to the task.[\[45\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaller1705iv-50) Through the club, Hooke met [Seth Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seth_Ward_\(bishop_of_Salisbury\) "Seth Ward (bishop of Salisbury)") (the University's [Savilian Professor of Astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savilian_Professor_of_Astronomy "Savilian Professor of Astronomy")) and developed for Ward a mechanism that improved the regularity of pendulum clocks used for astronomical time-keeping.[\[46\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin201711-51) Hooke characterised his Oxford days as the foundation of his lifelong passion for science.[\[47\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJardine200381-52) The friends he made there, particularly [Christopher Wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Wren "Christopher Wren"), were important to him throughout his career. Willis introduced Hooke to [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle"), who the club sought to attract to Oxford.[\[48\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin201715-53)

In 1655, Boyle moved to Oxford and Hooke became nominally his assistant but in practice his co-experimenter.[\[48\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin201715-53) Boyle had been working on gas pressures; the possibility a vacuum might exist despite [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle")'s maxim "[Nature abhors a vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horror_vacui_\(physics\) "Horror vacui (physics)")" had just [begun to be considered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelista_Torricelli#Barometer "Evangelista Torricelli"). Hooke developed an air pump for Boyle's experiments rather than use [Ralph Greatorex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Greatorex "Ralph Greatorex")'s pump, which Hooke considered as "too gross to perform any great matter".[\[49\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFulton1960123-54) Hooke's engine enabled the development of the [eponymous law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyles_Law "Boyles Law") that was subsequently attributed to Boyle;[\[50\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin201719-55)[\[f\]](#cite_note-57) Hooke had a particularly keen eye and was an adept mathematician, neither of which applied to Boyle. Hooke taught Boyle [Euclid's *Elements*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_Elements "Euclid's Elements") and [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes "Descartes")'s *[Principles of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principles_of_Philosophy "Principles of Philosophy")*;[\[9\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAubrey1898411-10) it also caused them to recognise [fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire "Fire") as a chemical reaction and not, as Aristotle taught, a [fundamental element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_element "Classical element") of nature.[\[52\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood200319,_20-58)

### Royal Society

Hooke's scientific work while employed by the Society is summarised in the section [§ Science](#Science), below.

According to Henry Robinson, Librarian of The Royal Society in 1935:

> Without his weekly experiments and prolific work the Society could scarcely have survived, or, at least, would have developed in a quite different way. It is scarcely an exaggeration to say that he was, historically, the creator of the Royal Society.[\[53\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobinson1935[httpsarchiveorgdetailsdiaryofroberthoo0000robepagen25mode1up_xx]-59)

The [Royal Society for the Improvement of Natural Knowledge by Experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society")[\[g\]](#cite_note-60) was founded in 1660 and given its [Royal Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Charter "Royal Charter") in July 1662\.[\[54\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJardine200396-61) On 5 November 1661, [Robert Moray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moray "Robert Moray") proposed the appointment of a curator to furnish the society with experiments and this was unanimously passed and Hooke was named on Boyle's recommendation.[\[9\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAubrey1898411-10) The society did not have a reliable income to fully fund the post of 'curator of experiments' but in 1664, [John Cutler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_John_Cutler,_1st_Baronet "Sir John Cutler, 1st Baronet") settled an annual gratuity of £50 on the society to found a "Mechanick" lectureship at Gresham College[\[55\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaller1705xi-62) on the understanding the society would appoint Hooke to this task.[\[56\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood200329-63) On 27 June 1664, Hooke was confirmed to the office and on 11 January 1665, he was named Curator by Office for life with an annual salary of £80,[\[h\]](#cite_note-64) consisting of £30 from the society and Cutler's £50 annuity.[\[56\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood200329-63)[\[i\]](#cite_note-65)

In June 1663, Hooke was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (FRS).[\[57\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood200328-66) On 20 March 1665, he was also appointed [Gresham Professor of Geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_Professor_of_Geometry "Gresham Professor of Geometry").[\[58\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood200330-67)[\[59\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJardine2003100-68) On 13 September 1667, Hooke became acting secretary of the society[\[60\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003241-69) and on 19 December 1677, he was appointed its joint secretary.[\[61\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003247-70)

### Personality, relationships, health, and death

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/14/Acta_Eruditorum_-_III_fisica%2C_1707_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13369403.jpg/250px-Acta_Eruditorum_-_III_fisica%2C_1707_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13369403.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Acta_Eruditorum_-_III_fisica,_1707_–_BEIC_13369403.jpg)

Illustration from *The posthumous works of Robert Hooke...* published in *[Acta Eruditorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acta_Eruditorum "Acta Eruditorum")*, 1707

Although [John Aubrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Aubrey "John Aubrey") described Hooke as a person of "great virtue and goodness".[\[62\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDrake19965-71) much has been written about the unpleasant side of Hooke's personality. According to his first biographer Richard Waller, Hooke was "in person, but despicable", and "melancholy, mistrustful, and jealous".[\[63\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaller1705xxvii-72) Waller's comments influenced other writers for more than 200 years such that many books and articles – especially biographies of [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") – portray Hooke as a disgruntled, selfish, anti-social curmudgeon. For example, Arthur Berry said Hooke "claimed credit for most of the scientific discoveries of the time".[\[64\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBerry1898221-73) Sullivan wrote he was "positively unscrupulous" and had an "uneasy apprehensive vanity" in dealings with Newton.[\[65\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTESullivan193835–37-74) Manuel described Hooke as "cantankerous, envious, vengeful".[\[66\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEManuel1968138-75) According to More, Hooke had both a "cynical temperament" and a "caustic tongue".[\[67\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMore193494–95-76) Andrade was more sympathetic but still described Hooke as "difficult", "suspicious" and "irritable".[\[68\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAndrarde195056–57-77) In October 1675, the Council of the Royal Society considered a motion to expel Hooke because of an attack he made on [Christiaan Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens "Christiaan Huygens") over [scientific priority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_priority "Scientific priority") in watch design but it did not pass.[\[69\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003199,_200-78) According to Hooke's biographer Ellen Drake:

> if one studies the intellectual milieu of the time, the controversies and rivalries of the type in which he was involved seem almost to be the rule rather than the exception. And Hooke's reaction to such controversy involving his own discoveries and inventions seems mild in comparison to the behaviour of some of his contemporaries".[\[70\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDrake1996104-79)

The publication of Hooke's diary in 1935[\[71\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobinson1935-80) revealed previously unknown details about his social and familial relationships. His biographer Margaret 'Espinasse said: "the picture which is usually painted of Hooke as a morose ... recluse is completely false".[\[72\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTE'Espinasse1956106-81) He interacted with noted artisans such as clock-maker [Thomas Tompion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Tompion "Thomas Tompion")[\[73\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003145-82) and instrument-maker Christopher Cocks (Cox).[\[74\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003159-83) Hooke often met Christopher Wren, with whom he shared many interests, and had a lasting friendship with John Aubrey. His diaries also make frequent reference to meetings at coffeehouses and taverns, as well as to dinners with Robert Boyle. On many occasions, Hooke took tea with his lab assistant Harry Hunt. Although he largely lived alone – apart from the servants who ran his home – his niece Grace Hooke and his cousin Tom Giles lived with him for some years as children.[\[75\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003227-84)

Hooke never married. According to his diary, Hooke had a sexual relationship with his niece Grace, after she had turned 16\. Grace had been in his custody since the age of 10\.[\[76\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003140-85)[\[77\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJardine2003257-86) He also had sexual relations with several maids and housekeepers. Hooke's biographer Stephen Inwood considers Grace to have been the love of his life, and he was devastated when she died in 1687\. Inwood also mentions "The age difference between him and Grace was commonplace and would not have upset his contemporaries as it does us". The incestuous relationship would nevertheless have been frowned upon and tried by an ecclesiastical court had it been discovered: it was not, however, a capital felony after 1660\.[\[78\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003138–140-87)[\[j\]](#cite_note-89)

Since childhood, Hooke suffered from [migraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migraine "Migraine"), [tinnitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tinnitus "Tinnitus"), dizziness, and bouts of [insomnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insomnia "Insomnia");[\[80\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood2003133–138-90) he also had a spinal deformity that was consistent with a diagnosis of [Scheuermann's kyphosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheuermann's_kyphosis "Scheuermann's kyphosis"), giving him in middle and later years a "thin and crooked body, over-large head and protruding eyes".[\[81\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood200310-91) Approaching these in a scientific spirit, he experimented with self-medication, diligently recording symptoms, substances, and effects in his diary. He regularly used [sal ammoniac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sal_ammoniac "Sal ammoniac"), emetics, laxatives, and opium, which appear to have had an increasing effect on his physical and mental health over time.[\[82\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJardine2003216,_217-92)

Hooke died in London on 3 March 1703, having been blind and bedridden during the last year of his life. A chest containing £8,000 in money and gold was found in his room at [Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_College "Gresham College").[\[83\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood20034-93)[\[k\]](#cite_note-94) His library contained over 3,000 books in Latin, French, Italian, and English.[\[83\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood20034-93) Although he had talked of leaving a generous bequest to the Royal Society, which would have given his name to a library, laboratory, and lectures, no will was found and the money passed to a cousin named Elizabeth Stephens.[\[84\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood20033-95) Hooke was buried at [St Helen's Church, Bishopsgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Helen's_Church,_Bishopsgate "St Helen's Church, Bishopsgate"), in the [City of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London "City of London")[\[85\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin2017218-96) but the precise location of his grave is unknown.

## Science

Hooke's role at the Royal Society was to demonstrate experiments from his own methods or at the suggestion of members. Among his earliest demonstrations were discussions of the nature of air and the implosion of glass bubbles that had been sealed with enclosed hot air.[\[57\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood200328-66) He also demonstrated that a dog could be kept alive with its [thorax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorax "Thorax") opened, provided air was pumped in and out of its lungs.[\[86\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood200343-97)[\[l\]](#cite_note-99) He noted the difference between [venous blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venous_blood "Venous blood") and [arterial blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arterial_blood "Arterial blood"), and thus demonstrated that the *Pabulum vitae* ("food of life")[\[m\]](#cite_note-101) and *flammae* \[flames\] were the same thing.[\[89\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaller1705ix-102)[\[90\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTELong_Hall1976156-103) There were also experiments on gravity, the falling of objects, the weighing of bodies, the measurement of [barometric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barometric_pressure "Barometric pressure") at different heights, and the movement of [pendulums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum "Pendulum") up to 200 ft (61 m) long.[\[89\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaller1705ix-102) His biographer Margaret 'Espinasse described him as England's first [meteorologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorologist "Meteorologist"), in her description of his essay *Method for making a history of the weather*.[\[91\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTE'Espinasse1956[httpsarchiveorgdetailsroberthooke0000margpage50mode2upviewtheater_50]-104) (Hooke specifies that a thermometer, a [hygrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hygrometer "Hygrometer"), a wind gauge, and a record sheet be used for proper weather records.[\[92\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHooke1734173–179-105)[\[n\]](#cite_note-106))

### Astronomy

[![Hooke's drawing of the planet Saturn](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Saturn_Robert_Hooke_1666.jpg/250px-Saturn_Robert_Hooke_1666.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saturn_Robert_Hooke_1666.jpg)

Hooke noted the shadows (a and b) cast by both the globe and the rings on each other in this drawing of Saturn.

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0c/Moon_Micrographia_Hooke.png/250px-Moon_Micrographia_Hooke.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Moon_Micrographia_Hooke.png)

Drawings of the Moon and the Pleiades from Hooke's *Micrographia*

In May 1664, using a 12 ft (3.7 m) [refracting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracting_telescope "Refracting telescope"), Hooke observed the [Great Red Spot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Red_Spot "Great Red Spot") of [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter "Jupiter") for two hours as it moved across the planet's face. In March 1665, he published his findings and from them, the Italian astronomer [Giovanni Cassini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Cassini "Giovanni Cassini") calculated the [rotation period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_period "Rotation period") of Jupiter to be nine hours and fifty-five minutes.[\[93\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEInwood200351-107)

One of the most-challenging problems Hooke investigated was the measurement of the distance from Earth to a star other than the Sun. Hooke selected the star [Gamma Draconis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_Draconis "Gamma Draconis") and chose the method of [parallax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallax "Parallax") determination. In 1669, after several months of observing, Hooke believed the desired result had been achieved. It is now known his equipment was far too imprecise to obtain an accurate measurement.[\[94\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHirshfeld2001[httpsarchiveorgdetailsparallax00alanpage144_144–149]-108)

Hooke's *Micrographia* contains illustrations of the [Pleiades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleiades "Pleiades") star cluster and [lunar craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_craters "Lunar craters"). He conducted experiments to investigate the formation of these craters and concluded their existence meant the Moon must have its own gravity, a radical departure from the contemporaneous [Aristotelian celestial model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity#Ancient_world "Gravity").[\[95\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGribbinGribbin201757-109) He also was an early observer of the [rings of Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rings_of_Saturn "Rings of Saturn"),[\[96\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlexander1962108–109-110) and discovered one of the first-observed [double stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_star "Double star"), [Gamma Arietis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_Arietis "Gamma Arietis") (a [visual binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_binary "Visual binary")), in 1664\.[\[97\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAitken1935[httpsarchiveorgdetailsdliernet15387page1mode1up_1]-111)
