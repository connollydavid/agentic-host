From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Type of telescope design (astronomy magnifier)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/23/Gregorian_telescope.svg/500px-Gregorian_telescope.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Gregorian_telescope.svg)

Diagram of the lightpath through a Gregorian telescope

The **Gregorian telescope** is a type of [reflecting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_telescope "Reflecting telescope") designed by Scottish mathematician and [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer "Astronomer") [James Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gregory_\(astronomer_and_mathematician\) "James Gregory (astronomer and mathematician)") in the 17th century, and first built in 1673 by [Robert Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke "Robert Hooke"). James Gregory was a contemporary of [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), and both often worked simultaneously on similar projects. Gregory's design was published in 1663 and pre-dates the first practical reflecting telescope, the [Newtonian telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_telescope "Newtonian telescope"), built by Sir Isaac Newton in 1668\.[\[1\]](#cite_note-books.google.com-1) However, Gregory's design was only a theoretical description, and he never actually constructed the telescope. It was not successfully built until five years after [Newton's first reflecting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_reflector "Newton's reflector").

## History

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Gregorian_telescope&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")\]

The Gregorian telescope is named after the James Gregory design, which appeared in his 1663 publication *Optica Promota* (The Advance of Optics). Similar theoretical designs have been found in the writings of [Bonaventura Cavalieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonaventura_Cavalieri "Bonaventura Cavalieri") (*Lo Specchio Ustorio* (On Burning Mirrors), 1632[\[2\]](#cite_note-2)) and [Marin Mersenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Mersenne "Marin Mersenne") (*L'harmonie universalle*, 1636[\[3\]](#cite_note-3)). Gregory's early attempts to build the telescope failed, since he had no practical skill himself and could find no optician capable of actually constructing one.[\[4\]](#cite_note-4) It was not until ten years after Gregory's publication, aided by the interest of experimental scientist [Robert Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke "Robert Hooke"), that a working instrument was created.[\[5\]](#cite_note-5) The early Scottish optician and telescope maker [James Short](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Short_\(mathematician\) "James Short (mathematician)") built Gregorian telescopes with parabolic mirrors made from the highly reflective [speculum metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speculum_metal "Speculum metal").

## Design

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Gregorian_telescope&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Design")\]

The Gregorian telescope consists of two [concave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curved_mirror#Concave_mirrors "Curved mirror") [mirrors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror "Mirror"): the primary mirror (a concave [paraboloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraboloid "Paraboloid")) collects the light and brings it to a [focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_\(optics\) "Focus (optics)") *before* the secondary mirror (a concave [ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipsoid "Ellipsoid")), where it is reflected back through a hole in the centre of the [primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_mirror "Primary mirror"), and thence out the bottom end of the instrument, where it can be viewed with the aid of the [eyepiece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyepiece "Eyepiece").

The Gregorian design solved the problem of viewing the image in a reflector by allowing the observer to stand behind the primary mirror. This design of telescope renders an [erect image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erect_image "Erect image"), making it useful for terrestrial observations. It also works as a [telephoto lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephoto_lens "Telephoto lens") with its tube much shorter than the system's actual [focal length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focal_length "Focal length").

The design was largely superseded by the [Cassegrain telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassegrain_reflector "Cassegrain reflector") as equivalent focal length is greater than the physical focal length which provides better illumination uniformity.[\[6\]](#cite_note-6) It is still used for some [spotting scopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotting_scope "Spotting scope") because this design creates an erect image without the need for prisms. The [Steward Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steward_Observatory "Steward Observatory") Mirror Lab has been making mirrors for large Gregorian telescopes at least since 1985\.

In the Gregorian design, the primary mirror creates a real image before the secondary mirror. This allows for a [field stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_stop "Field stop") to be placed at this location, so that the light from outside the field of view does not reach the secondary mirror. This is a major advantage for solar telescopes, where a field stop (Gregorian stop) can reduce the amount of heat reaching the secondary mirror and subsequent optical components. The Solar Optical Telescope on the [Hinode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinode_\(satellite\) "Hinode (satellite)") satellite is one example of this design.

For [amateur telescope makers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_telescope_making "Amateur telescope making") the Gregorian can be less difficult to fabricate than a Cassegrain because the concave secondary is [Foucault-testable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foucault_knife-edge_test "Foucault knife-edge test") like the primary, which is not the case with the Cassegrain's convex secondary.

## Examples

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Gregorian_telescope&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Examples")\]

- The [MeerKAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MeerKAT "MeerKAT"), the [Green Bank Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Bank_Telescope "Green Bank Telescope"), the [Arecibo Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arecibo_Observatory "Arecibo Observatory"), and the [Allen Telescope Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Telescope_Array "Allen Telescope Array") are all radio telescopes employing off-axis Gregorian optics.

- The [Vatican Advanced Technology Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vatican_Advanced_Technology_Telescope "Vatican Advanced Technology Telescope"), the [Magellan telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magellan_telescopes "Magellan telescopes"), and the [Large Binocular Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Binocular_Telescope "Large Binocular Telescope") use Gregorian optics.

- The [Giant Magellan Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_Magellan_Telescope "Giant Magellan Telescope") will also use Gregorian optics.

- The [NSF's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation "National Science Foundation") [Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_K._Inouye_Solar_Telescope "Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope")
