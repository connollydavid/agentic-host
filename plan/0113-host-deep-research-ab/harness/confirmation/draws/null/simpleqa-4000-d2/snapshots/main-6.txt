**James Gregory** (November 1638 – October 1675) was a Scottish mathematician and [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer "Astronomer"). His surname is sometimes spelt as **Gregorie**, the original [Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_language "Scots language") spelling. He described an early practical design for the [reflecting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_telescope "Reflecting telescope") – the [Gregorian telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_telescope "Gregorian telescope") – and made advances in [trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry"), discovering [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series "Infinite series") representations for several trigonometric functions.

In his book *Geometriae Pars Universalis* (1668)[\[1\]](#cite_note-geometriae-1) Gregory gave both the first published statement and proof of the [fundamental theorem of the calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_the_calculus "Fundamental theorem of the calculus") (stated from a geometric point of view, and only for a special class of the curves considered by later versions of the theorem), for which he was acknowledged by [Isaac Barrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Barrow "Isaac Barrow").[\[2\]](#cite_note-2)[\[3\]](#cite_note-3)[\[4\]](#cite_note-4)[\[5\]](#cite_note-5)[\[6\]](#cite_note-6)[\[7\]](#cite_note-7)\[*[excessive citations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#Bundling_citations "Wikipedia:Citing sources")*\]

## Biography

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=James_Gregory_\(mathematician\)&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Biography")\]

Gregory was born in 1638\. His mother Janet was the daughter of [Jean and David Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Guild "Jean Guild") and his father was John Gregory,[\[8\]](#cite_note-8) an [Episcopalian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopalianism_in_the_Church_of_Scotland "Episcopalianism in the Church of Scotland") [Church of Scotland minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Scotland_minister "Church of Scotland minister"), James was youngest of their three children and he was born in the [manse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manse "Manse") at [Drumoak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumoak "Drumoak"), [Aberdeenshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeenshire_\(historic\) "Aberdeenshire (historic)"), and was initially educated at home by his mother, Janet Anderson (\~1600–1668). It was his mother who endowed Gregory with his appetite for [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), her uncle – [Alexander Anderson (1582–1619)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Anderson_\(mathematician\) "Alexander Anderson (mathematician)") – having been a pupil and editor of French mathematician [Viète](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viète "Viète"). After his father's death in 1651 his elder brother David took over responsibility for his education. He attended [Aberdeen Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen_Grammar_School "Aberdeen Grammar School"), and then [Marischal College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marischal_College "Marischal College") from 1653 to 1657, graduating AM in 1657\.

In 1663 he went to London, meeting [John Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Collins_\(mathematician\) "John Collins (mathematician)") and fellow Scot [Robert Moray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moray "Robert Moray"), one of the founders of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society"). In 1664 he departed for the [University of Padua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Padua "University of Padua"), in the [Venetian Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Republic "Venetian Republic"), passing through [Flanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flanders "Flanders"), Paris and Rome on his way. At Padua he lived in the house of his countryman [James Caddenhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Caddenhead?action=edit&redlink=1 "James Caddenhead (page does not exist)"), the professor of philosophy, and he was taught by [Stefano Angeli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stefano_Angeli "Stefano Angeli"). The geometrical treatises he published in Padua, *Vera circuli et hyperbolæ quadratura* (1667) and *Geometriæ pars universalis* (1668), testify to the deep influence the works of Angeli and other leading Italian mathematicians, such as [Gabriele Manfredi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriele_Manfredi "Gabriele Manfredi"), exerted on him.[\[9\]](#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWhiteside2008524-9)

Upon his return to London in 1668 he was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society"), before travelling to [St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Andrews "St Andrews") in late 1668 to take up his post as the first [Regius Professor of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regius_Professor_of_Mathematics "Regius Professor of Mathematics") at the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews "University of St Andrews"), a position created for him by [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England "Charles II of England"), probably upon the request of Robert Moray. There at the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews "University of St Andrews"), he laid the first meridian line across the floor of his lab in 1673, which was 200 years prior to the Greenwich Meridian being established, and thus "arguably making St Andrews the place where time began".[\[10\]](#cite_note-10)[\[11\]](#cite_note-11)

He was successively professor at the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews "University of St Andrews") and the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh").

He had married Mary, daughter of [George Jameson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Jamesone "George Jamesone"), painter, and widow of John Burnet of Elrick, Aberdeen; their son James was Professor of Physics at [King's College, Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Aberdeen "King's College, Aberdeen"). He was the grandfather of [John Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gregory_\(moralist\) "John Gregory (moralist)") (FRS 1756); uncle of [David Gregorie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Gregory_\(mathematician\) "David Gregory (mathematician)") (FRS 1692) and brother of [David Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Gregory_\(physician\) "David Gregory (physician)") (1627–1720), a physician and inventor.

About a year after assuming the Chair of Mathematics at [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh "Edinburgh"), James Gregory suffered a stroke while viewing the moons of Jupiter with his students. He died a few days later at the age of 36\.

### *Optica Promota*

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=James_Gregory_\(mathematician\)&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Optica Promota")\]

In the *Optica Promota*, published in 1663, Gregory described his design for a [reflecting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_telescope "Reflecting telescope"), the "[Gregorian telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_telescope "Gregorian telescope")". He also described the method for using the [transit of Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_of_Venus "Transit of Venus") to measure the distance of the Earth from the Sun, which was later advocated by [Edmund Halley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Halley "Edmund Halley") and adopted as the basis of the first effective measurement of the [Astronomical Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_Unit "Astronomical Unit").

### *Vera Circuli et Hyperbolae Quadratura*

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=James_Gregory_\(mathematician\)&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Vera Circuli et Hyperbolae Quadratura")\]

Before he left Padua, Gregory published *Vera Circuli et Hyperbolae Quadratura* (1667) in which he approximated the areas of the [circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle "Circle") and [hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbola "Hyperbola") with convergent series:

: \[James Gregory\] cannot be denied the authorship of many curious theorems on the relation of the circle to [inscribed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_figure "Inscribed figure") and [circumscribed polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangential_polygon "Tangential polygon"), and their relation to each other. By means of these theorems he gives with infinitely less trouble than by the usual calculations, … the measure of the circle and hyperbola (and consequently the construction of [logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithm "Logarithm")) to more than twenty decimal places. Following the example of [Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens "Christiaan Huygens"), he also gave constructions of straight lines equal to the [arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_\(geometry\) "Arc (geometry)") of the circle, and whose error is still less.[\[12\]](#cite_note-12)

"The first proof of the [fundamental theorem of calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_calculus "Fundamental theorem of calculus") and the discovery of the [Taylor series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series "Taylor series") can both be attributed to him."[\[13\]](#cite_note-13)[\[14\]](#cite_note-14)

The book was reprinted in 1668 with an appendix, *Geometriae Pars*, in which Gregory explained how the volumes of [solids of revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solids_of_revolution "Solids of revolution") could be determined.

## Gregorian telescope

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=James_Gregory_\(mathematician\)&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Gregorian telescope")\]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/23/Gregorian_telescope.svg/500px-Gregorian_telescope.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Gregorian_telescope.svg)

Diagram of a Gregorian reflecting telescope.

Main article: [Gregorian telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_telescope "Gregorian telescope")

In his 1663 *Optica Promota*, James Gregory described his [reflecting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_telescope "Reflecting telescope") which has come to be known by his name, the Gregorian telescope. Gregory pointed out that a reflecting telescope with a [parabolic mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_reflector "Parabolic reflector") would correct [spherical aberration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_aberration "Spherical aberration") as well as the [chromatic aberration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_aberration "Chromatic aberration") seen in [refracting telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracting_telescope "Refracting telescope").[\[15\]](#cite_note-15) In his design he also placed a concave [secondary mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_mirror "Secondary mirror") with an elliptical surface past the focal point of the parabolic [primary mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_mirror "Primary mirror"), reflecting the image back through a hole in the primary mirror where it could be conveniently viewed. According to his own confession, Gregory had no practical skill and he could find no optician capable of actually constructing one.[\[16\]](#cite_note-16)

The telescope design attracted the attention of several people in the scientific establishment such as [Robert Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke "Robert Hooke"), the Oxford physicist who eventually built the telescope 10 years later, and Sir [Robert Moray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moray "Robert Moray"), [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath "Polymath") and founding member of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society").

The Gregorian telescope design is rarely used today, as other types of reflecting telescopes are known to be more efficient for standard applications. Gregorian optics are also used in [radio telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_telescopes "Radio telescopes") such as [Arecibo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arecibo_Observatory "Arecibo Observatory"), which features a "Gregorian dome".[\[17\]](#cite_note-17)

## Mathematics

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=James_Gregory_\(mathematician\)&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Mathematics")\]

The following excerpt is from the *[Pantologia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantologia "Pantologia"). A new (cabinet) cyclopædia* (1813)

> Mr. James Gregory was a man of a very acute and penetrating genius. ...The most brilliant part of his character was that of his mathematical genius as an inventor, which was of the first order; as will appear by... his inventions and discoveries \[which include\] quadrature of the circle and hyperbola, by an infinite converging series; his method for the transformation of curves; a geometrical demonstration of [Lord Brouncker's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Brouncker,_2nd_Viscount_Brouncker "William Brouncker, 2nd Viscount Brouncker") series for squaring the hyperbola—his demonstration that the meridian line is analogous to a scale of logarithmic tangents of the half complements of the latitude; he also invented and demonstrated geometrically, by help of the hyperbola, a very simple converging series for making the logarithms; he sent to [Mr. Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Collins_\(mathematician\) "John Collins (mathematician)") the solution of the famous [Keplerian problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler_problem "Kepler problem") by an infinite series; he discovered a method of drawing [Tangents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent "Tangent") to curves geometrically, without any previous calculations; a rule for the direct and inverse method of tangents, which stands upon the same principle (of [exhaustions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_exhaustion "Method of exhaustion")) with that of [fluxions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_Fluxions "Method of Fluxions"), and differs not much from it in the manner of application; a series for the length of the arc of a circle from the tangent, and vice versa; as also for the secant and logarithmic tangent and secant, and vice versa. These, with others, for measuring the length of the elliptic and hyperbolic curves, were sent to Mr. Collins, in return for some received from him of [Newton's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), in which he followed the elegant example of this author, in delivering his series in simple terms, independent of each other.[\[18\]](#cite_note-18)

## Other work

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=James_Gregory_\(mathematician\)&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Other work")\]

In a letter of 1671 to [John Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Collins_\(mathematician\) "John Collins (mathematician)"), Gregory gives the [power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_series "Power series") expansion of the seven functions (using modern notation) arctan⁡x (often called [Gregory's series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory's_series "Gregory's series")), tan⁡x, sec⁡x, log⁡sec⁡x, the inverse [Gudermannian function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudermannian_function "Gudermannian function") log⁡tan⁡12(x\+12π), arcsec⁡(2ex), and the Gudermannian function 2arctan⁡ex−12π.[\[19\]](#cite_note-19)

There is evidence that he discovered the method of taking higher derivatives in order to compute a power series, which was not discovered by Taylor until 1715, but did not publish his results, thinking he had only rediscovered "Mr. Newton's universal method," which was based on a different technique.[\[20\]](#cite_note-20)

James Gregory discovered the [diffraction grating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_grating "Diffraction grating") by passing [sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight "Sunlight") through a bird [feather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feather "Feather") and observing the diffraction pattern produced.[\[21\]](#cite_note-21) In particular he observed the splitting of sunlight into its component colours – this occurred a year after Newton had done the same with a [prism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prism_\(optics\) "Prism (optics)") and the phenomenon was still highly controversial.

A round wheel is unsuitable for irregular surfaces, and Gregory devised an appropriate "adaptable wheel" using a *Gregory transformation*.[\[22\]](#cite_note-22)

Gregory, an enthusiastic supporter of Newton, later had much friendly correspondence with him and incorporated his ideas into his own teaching, ideas which at that time were controversial and considered quite revolutionary.

The crater [Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_\(lunar_crater\) "Gregory (lunar crater)") on the Moon is named after him. He was the uncle of mathematician [David Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Gregory_\(mathematician\) "David Gregory (mathematician)").
